ABSTRACT
Applying Large Language Models (LLMs) to graph-structured data usually in-
volves multi-step pipelines in which textual node attributes are compressed into
single tokens and further processed by GNNs, discarding most of their semantic
content. We introduce the Graph Transformer Language Model (GTLM), which
enables a pretrained LLM to process graph topology natively and removes this
bottleneck entirely. GTLM injects graph-aware attention biases directly into the
LLM’s attention modules, adding only 0.015% structure-related parameters rela-
tive to the base model. Training updates only the structural parameters together
with a LoRA adapter on the base model. We prove that our bidirectional atten-
tion prefix is permutation-equivariant over nodes and that GTLM reduces exactly
to the pretrained model when no graph is present. Having no global node order-
ing, GTLM shows no positional degradation and does notget lost in the middle:
needle-in-a-graph accuracy stays flat from 1k to 64k tokens and 4× past the train-
ing length, while an identically trained flat-text baseline collapses. Comprehen-
sive evaluations show that a GTLM matches or exceeds domain-specific state-of-
the-art models on text-attributed graph benchmarks, GraphRAG on WebQSP, and
molecular benchmarks, while meaningfully improving over strong baselines on
GraphQA. We further show that GTLM’s attention heads implicitly learn to simu-
late message passing, explaining its strength on algorithmic tasks. Together, these
results suggest that a minimally adapted pretrained LLM can serve as a general
backbone for graph learning.
1 INTRODUCTION
Many real-world problems involve interconnected structures which Large Language Models (LLMs)
have to work around due to their inherent inability to perceive structure. The standard way to give
such data to an LLM is to serialize the graph into text (Fatemi et al., 2024). Serialization introduces
an arbitrary node order, so the output depends on a choice that carries no meaning.
However, from an architectural point of view, a decoder-only LLM is already a Graph Transformer
(GT) (Ying et al., 2021): its input is a path graph over tokens, and the positional encodings under the
causal mask are the structure that its attention sees. Moving from a path to any graph should mean
changing the way attention sees structure, not adding a separate graph encoder to the model.
Most existing approaches do the latter. Graph-Tokenizing LLMs (GTokenLLMs) (Zhang et al.,
2026) typically rely on disjointed, multi-step pipelines. These frameworks condense each text-
attributed node into a single token via a preliminary encoder, optionally process these representations
with Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), and pass the representations into rigid serialization templates.
While effective on some benchmarks, this paradigm introduces significant bottlenecks: the fixed
input formats constrain versatility, and the lossy compression of rich textual data into solitary tokens
prevents the model from accessing fine-grained, node-level information.
In this paper, we introduce the Graph Transformer Language Model (GTLM), a novel architecture
that addresses the limitations of multi-step pipelines by integrating structural information directly

2 RELATEDWORK
2.1 LARGELANGUAGEMODELS FORGRAPHS
Initial approaches to graph-LLM integration relied on explicit text serialization (e.g., Fatemi et al.,
2024), which inherently suffers from poor graph-aware reasoning and rapid context exhaustion. To
mitigate these constraints, research pioneered by Perozzi et al. (2024) shifted toward multimodal
graph alignment, a paradigm further advanced by models such as GraphGPT (Tang et al., 2024),
LLaGA (Chen et al., 2024), and RGLM (Zhang et al., 2026). These architectures project graph
nodes into “soft tokens” within the LLM’s embedding space to facilitate structural processing.
Despite their efficiency, these alignment-based methods introduce a significantcompressive bot-
tleneck: rich, multi-sentence node attributes are condensed into solitary dense vectors, abstracting

2.2 STRUCTURALENCODINGS INGRAPHTRANSFORMERS
Since Transformers were adapted to graphs (Vaswani et al., 2017; Yuan et al., 2025), structural in-
jection typically falls into two categories:AbsolutePositional Embeddings (APEs), which modify
initial node features using structural priors, andRelativePositional Encodings (RPEs), which in-
fer topology via additive biases applied to raw attention scores. Our architecture relies on RPEs,
building upon three foundational methods.
First,Shortest Path Distances (SPD)index a learnable scalar bias based on the discrete shortest
path distance between any node pair, natively modulating token interaction strength based on struc-
tural proximity without message passing (Ying et al., 2021). Second,Relative Random Walk Prob-
abilities (RRWP)capture complex structural dynamics usingK-step walk probabilitiesP u,v ∈R K
derived from the graph’s transition matrix (Ma et al., 2023).
Finally, to capture directed graph topology, theMagnetic Laplacianutilizes a matrix that encodes

3 METHODOLOGY
To unify the graph and text modalities, we represent all data through an underlying graph structure
where nodes (and optionally edges) consist of text sequences. The entire input graph is processed by
a modified LLM, inspired by GTs, which we callGraph Transformer Language Model (GTLM).
It accounts for both the global topology and the relative positions of text tokens within each node.

3.3 THEORETICALPROPERTIES OFGTLM
Our proposed GTLM architecture satisfies two fundamental properties that successfully bridge the
theoretical gap between Graph Transformers and Large Language Models. We provide proofs for
both theoretical property claims, as well as empirical results to verify them in Appendix B.
Property 1—Prefix Node Permutation Equivariance:The internal representations and final out-
puts of GTLM are equivariant with respect to the serialization order of the prefix nodes.
Explanation:When flattening the nodes of a graph into a sequence, their order does not affect the
attention scores between any two tokens, because the attention score depends only on the position
ID (which is independent of the node order, due to the resetting of the RoPE ID) and the relative
bias at the node level, which is by definition invariant under permutations.

Property 2—Pretraining Backward Compatibility:For any graphGconsisting of a single node
(representing a standard text sequence), the GTLM forward pass is mathematically identical to that
of the unmodified base LLM.
Explanation:Since the condition that the bias is zero on the diagonal of the node-level bias matrix
is imposed by definition, the bias is a null operation in a single-node graph, as is resetting the RoPE
IDs. Finally, the full input is causally masked, since the only node is the target node—making the
architecture mathematically equivalent to the base LLM.
4 EXPERIMENTS
We conduct comprehensive experiments across a diverse set of established benchmarks. Further-
more, we conduct a thorough ablation study (Section 4.6) evaluating the importance of each at-
tention bias type. Unless stated otherwise,Llama-3.2-1B(Meta AI, 2024) served as the base
model, and a Low-Rank Adapter (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2021) was used. Specific details about the
training setup and hyperparameters for each experiment can be found in Appendices C–I. Each re-
sult that reports standard deviations was measured using three random seeds; otherwise only one
was used. Our base-model scaling analysis (Appendix J) finds that performance is mostly saturated
at 1B for classification tasks, while open-ended generation (such as WebQSP, CWQ, and ChEBI-20)
benefits from scaling to an 8B-parameter base model.

4.6 ABLATIONSTUDY

5 DISCUSSION
In this section, we look beyond the raw performance metrics to analyze the underlying mechanisms
and practical properties of GTLM. We demonstrate GTLM’s ability to simulate message passing,
and discuss its parameter efficiency, implementation, and future research directions.
SIMULATEDMESSAGEPASSING
To illustrate the internal mechanisms of GTLM, we fine-tune the 1-billion-parameter model on a
synthetic task where it must predict whether two nodes belong to the same connected component
(Figure 2a). By analyzing the resulting attention weights, we observe that many attention heads learn
to implicitly simulate message passing by leveraging our structural attention biases (Figure 2b).
Note that the attention scores between nodesKandUand the remaining nodes are near zero, while
the attention scores between same-clique nodes are higher. This experiment suggests that the GTLM
architecture can natively process graphs akin to dedicated GNNs or GTs. This property explains
the model’s exceptionally strong performance on the GraphQA benchmark, as it tests the model’s
algorithmic reasoning abilities. Further details about this experiment are provided in Appendix L.

EFFICIENCY
Parameter Efficiency.GTLM requires two types of parameters to be trained: (1) LoRA parameters,
and (2) bias-related parameters. LoRA adapters are known to add only a few percent of the total base
model parameters, specifically, around2%when training a 1-billion-parameter model with rank32.
On the other hand, our bias-related parameter count is almost negligible in comparison to the total
number of parameters in modern LLMs. In total, the 173,056 bias-related parameters—comprising
4,096 for SPD, 50,688 for RRWP, and 118,272 for the Magnetic Laplacian—account for only around
0.015%of the 1-billion-parameter base LLM.
Implementation.Since the graph bias is an additive term on the attention logits, it renders the
FlashAttention Dao et al. (2022) backend unusable for GTLM. Therefore, we implement the graph-
bias-compatible attention backend using a PyTorch FlexAttentionscore mod(Dong et al., 2025).
Unlike the naive eager approach, our implementation does not materialize the fullL×Latten-
tion matrixA, nor the full token-level bias matrix; it only instantiates the node-level bias matrix
[b(u, v)]u,v ∈R N×N per head and layer. We use a sequence-to-node index to perform a single
lookup of the appropriate bias value inside the kernel. Since each node contributes many tokens,
N≪L, and this removes theO(L 2)memory term the bias introduced. On our TAG benchmarks,
a forward+backward step costs around2×that of a standard LLM using Flash Attention on exactly
identical token sequences with no graph machinery, at exactly the same peak memory. Compared
with the eager implementation, the same step time is2-10×faster with substantially lower peak
memory, removing theO(L 2)bottleneck. Since the advantage only becomes meaningful at a scale
beyond a few hundred tokens, we select the appropriate backend per experiment. More information
and detailed scaling measurements are available in Appendix M.
FUTURERESEARCH
Generalist Foundation Models.Given our strong results across a wide variety of problem types
related to graphs and text, alongside the theoretical guarantee that GTLM reduces to a standard
LLM with text-only inputs, we believe GTLM to be a strong architecture for building a generalist
foundation model for these combined modalities. This endeavor would require a large-scale corpus
of various graph-related tasks for continual pretraining of the model, as well as an instruction-tuning
set with graph-conditioned questions to facilitate zero-shot interactions. Ultimately, such a system
could meaningfully accelerate progress in domains such as molecular discovery, logical reasoning,
or enterprise knowledge retrieval, where data fundamentally is structured as a graph.

6 CONCLUSION
We introducedGTLM, a novel architecture that natively unifies graph and text modalities without
relying on lossy node compression or disjointed multi-step pipelines. By mathematically aligning
structural graph attention biases with the prefix-LM masking strategy, GTLM retains exact backward
compatibility with its pretrained LLM foundation while achieving state-of-the-art performance on a
variety of benchmarks with structured data. Beyond standard benchmark performance, we show that
GTLM generalizes significantly better to longer out-of-distribution sequences than an LLM under a
needle-in-a-graph multi-hop retrieval setting.

B.2 EMPIRICALVERIFICATION
To complement the theoretical proofs provided above, we numerically verify both architectural guar-
antees. Because these properties are fundamental to the model’s forward pass and operate inde-
pendently of the learned weights, we evaluate them using the baseLlama-3.2-1Bmodel with
randomly initialized bias-related parameters.
Backward Compatibility.We construct a trivial graph consisting of a singletarget nodewith
no edges, assigned a randomly generated textual attribute. We perform a forward pass on this input
using both our GTLM architecture and the unmodified base Llama model. We monitor the maximum
absolute difference between the output logits of the two models. Across five independent trials, we
found this difference to be2.1×10 −5 ±8.3×10 −6. This delta is entirely within the expected
floating-point (FP32) accumulation error, numerically confirming exact backward compatibility.
Permutation Equivariance.We construct a multi-node test graph with a fixed topology. We then
generate two flattened input sequences by randomly permuting the serialization order of the prefix
nodes, while strictly preserving their textual attributes and the underlying graph structure. Both
permuted sequences are processed by GTLM, and the outputs are inversely permuted to align with
the canonical node order. We measure the maximum absolute difference in the final logits between
the two permuted sequences. The result we get across five independent trials is2.77×10−5 ±2.87×
10−6. These results confirm that our architecture is strictly equivariant to the arbitrary ordering of
nodes in the flattened sequence.

Learning rates.We utilize a differential learning rate strategy, assigning a higher rate to the struc-
tural attention bias parameters than to the LoRA adapters. This approach is motivated by two factors:
(1) the bias parameters are initialized randomly and must learn to map graph topology to the attention
space, whereas the LLM and adapters start from highly refined, pretrained weights, and (2) despite
their low parameter count, these biases are the primary mechanism for graph-awareness and require
a stronger gradient signal to converge. This ensures that the essential structural features are learned
effectively without being overshadowed by the larger, pretrained components during optimization.
D GTLMWITHALIBI AND DUALROPE
In order to demonstrate the generality of the GTLM architecture, specifically the per-node position
index resetting idea, we show that it is possible to implement withALiBianddual RoPE. We use
BLOOM (Scao et al., 2022) as base model for the former, and Gemma-3-1B (Gemma Team, 2025)
for the latter.
ALiBi.ALiBi adds a fixed distance penalty per head to the attention logits before softmax. GTLM’s
structural mask and graph bias are added in the same slot, so BLOOM’s attention requires no
changes. The only adaptation is to calculate ALiBi distances from GTLM’s node-position iden-
tifiers instead of the raw token order. This prevents the node order from acting as a spurious prior,
and the single-node graph still accurately reproduces standard BLOOM.
Dual RoPE.GTLM only changes the position IDs provided to RoPE, not the rotation, so it does
not depend on the underlying frequency of RoPE. The local (10k) and global (1M) Gemma-3 layers
rotate the same position identifiers per node. Each layer therefore encodes the order of tokens within
a node, and the graph bias, added identically in each layer, carries the structure between nodes. The
512-token sliding window is discarded because it follows the serialization order, not the graph. At
GraphQA sequence lengths (maximum 126 tokens), this change has no effect.
We provide the results for both and compare them to the results obtained with Llama-3.2-1B as the
base model (Table 9).

To investigate GTLM’s internal learned mechanisms (as discussed in Section 5), we designed a
controlled synthetic connectivity task. This section outlines the high-level methodology used to
isolate this emergent behavior, while full implementation details and hyperparameter configurations
are available in our project codebase.

L.1 DATASETGENERATION
We programmatically generated a dataset of random graphs to evaluate the model’s ability to de-
duce whether two arbitrary nodes belong to the same clique. Each graph is generated with two or
more isolated connected components. To prevent the model from relying on language priors, node
attributes are restricted to randomly assigned single letters.
To query the model, we introduce theprompt nodecontaining the textual question (e.g.,"Are the
nodes{x}and{y}connected? [Yes/No]"). This prompt node is explicitly linked
via directed edges to the two nodes in question. The dataset is strictly balanced to contain an equal
number of positive examples (targets share a connected component) and negative examples (targets
belong to disjoint components).
L.2 TRAINING ANDEVALUATIONSETUP
Model Configuration.We utilizeLlama-3.2-1Bas the base model, augmented with our full
suite of structural attention biases (SPD, RRWP, and Magnetic Laplacian). To ensure parameter
efficiency, the base model is frozen, and wedo notapply any Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA).
Optimization Strategy.We only train the randomly initialized bias-related parameters in our model.
This setup allows us to only observe the effects of our newly introduced structure-aware attention
biases on the model’s behavior.
Findings.We find that the model learns to solve this task with close to100%accuracy. We further
investigate the internal attention scores on one synthetic example and notice that multiple attention
heads have learned to simulate message passing. For the visualization in Section 5 we used one spe-
cific attention head which best illustrated the point, while many other attention heads also exhibited
a clear awareness of the graph structure—either through a similar “message passing” mechanism or
its inverse where disconnected nodes have high attention scores, etc.
In summary, this experiment suggests that our GTLM architecture has the capacity for simulating
the functionalities of dedicated GNNs and conventional GTs.
M FLEXATTENTION WITHGTLM
M.1 IMPLEMENTATION
The fused FlashAttention kernels are ineligible due to the additive per-head and per-layer graph bias.
The eager implementation materializes the bias at token level,(B, H, L, L), and this is what would
bound the sequence lengths GTLM could train on—not the architecture. However, our backend
keeps the bias at node level instead, storing only[b(u, v)] u,v ∈R N×N per head and layer and
gathering the relevant entry inside the kernel through a sequence-to-node indexπ:{1, . . . , L} →
{1, . . . , N}, so thescore modis a single lookup:
def score_mod(score, b, h, q, kv):
return score + node_bias[b, h, node_id[q], node_id[kv]]
Since the kernel never depends on how thenode biaswas produced, any bias type we define
is compatible with these kernels, making the model’s perception of graphs very easy to modify to
experiment with different bias types. The backend requiresLto be padded to a multiple of the
block size. Further, to overcome excessive kernel re-compiling, we pad the sequences and node
features into a few dozen predefined(N, L)buckets. Decoding uses a hybrid approach—due to the
aforementioned requirement of FlexAttention to have discrete shape buckets—we route the prefill
phase through our FlexAttention kernel, while each of the next autoregressive generation steps falls
back to eager attention.

To ensure full reproducibility and provide transparency regarding the computational requirements
of our architecture, we detail the hardware setup and empirical training times below.
Although some of our experiments used higher end graphics cards such as Nvidia’s B200 or B300,
all experiments can be conducted using a single NVIDIA A100 or H100 GPU (80GB VRAM). When
fine-tuning the baseLlama-3.2-1Bmodel equipped with our structural LoRA adapters, training
times on the individual GraphQA tasks ranged from 10 minutes (for standard graphs) to 40 minutes
(for incidence graphs). The more demanding Text-Attributed Graph (TAG) benchmarks required
between 6 and 8 hours of continuous training on an H100 GPU (with the exception ofOGBN-Arxiv,
which required around 20 hours). Other experiments were carried out on those higher-end graphics
cards, where training times ranged between 1 and 10 hours, depending on the task in question and
the training configuration. Finally, training the 3B and 8B model variants introduced approximately
2×and3×slowdowns, respectively.
