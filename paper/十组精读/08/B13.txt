              1  INTRODUCTION

                  Reinforcement learning (RL) with verifiable rewards has led to rapid advances in AI for math and
                    coding, where the correctness of the answers can be reliably measured and used as reward signals.
                    In comparison, for non-verifiable tasks such as open-ended generation, the evaluation criteria for
                 model responses can be diverse and sometimes subjective, which makes it difficult to define rewards
                      in RL. To resolve this issue, Gunjal et al. (2026) and Viswanathan et al. (2026) leverage rubric-
                  based RL, which first generates a set of rubrics that model responses should satisfy and then uses
                     the weighted coverage of these pre-defined rubrics as rewards.

                  However, such rubric-coverage-based reward is easily hacked. As shown in Figure 1, when a modelarXiv:2610.33512v1                   is only optimized towards maximizing the coverage of pre-defined rubrics, we observe that although
                     the model responses become more complete, their factual correctness and necessity decrease, and the
                   responses become more verbose. This makes them less preferred than base policy responses (Mah-
               moud et al., 2026). This reward hacking behavior is not surprising since rubrics as rewards currently
                  behave like a recall objective. The missing part is a precision objective: whether the content of a
                 model response is factually correct and necessary.

                 With this motivation, we propose FORK, which converts rubric-based rewards from a recall score
                      into an Fβ score that aggregates recall and precision via their weighted harmonic mean. Given a
                       set of rubrics, to measure the precision, we first divide a model response into a sequence of units
                 and then determine the necessity and factual correctness of each unit by prompting an LLM grader.

We demonstrate the effectiveness of FORK on both in-domain datasets (the test splits of RubricHub-
Medical and RubricHub-Science, Li et al. (2026)) and external benchmarks (HealthBench, Arora
et al. (2025) and ResearchQA, Yifei et al. (2026)). Compared to the standard coverage reward, FORK
significantly improves the factual correctness and necessity of responses (e.g., reducing the response
length by a factor of 2.6) while maintaining similar rubric coverage. These improvements also make
responses from rubric-based RL-trained models more preferred than base policy responses.

Further analysis supports the effectiveness of FORK. While rubric-coverage rewards (Gunjal et al.,
2026; Rezaei et al., 2025; Viswanathan et al., 2026) have a negative effect on satisfaction of absence-
based rubrics, our method improves satisfaction of both presence-based and absence-based rubrics,
enabling models to add necessary content and suppress irrelevant content at the same time. We also
verify that a model trained with our method remains preferred to the base model under different
judge prompts and remains more effective than other rubric-based RL methods when evaluated with
different LLM graders.

Our key contributions are:
  1. We propose FORK, which mitigates reward hacking in rubric-based RL training by incorporat-
     ing a precision objective into a standard coverage objective.
  2. We develop a reliable method for using an LLM grader to measure the precision of free-form
     responses given a set of rubrics to be satisfied.
  3. Experiments show that on multiple public benchmarks, compared to the standard coverage re-
    ward, FORK significantly improves precision while maintaining similar rubric coverage, which
     leads to substantial improvements in the preference score (i.e., win rate compared to the base
     policy). FORK also outperforms other rubric-based RL methods, achieving Pareto-optimal per-
    formance that balances rubric coverage and overall response quality.

2  RELATED WORKS

Rubric-Based Reinforcement Learning.  Rubric-based methods decompose response quality into
explicit, prompt-specific criteria, providing structured supervision for tasks without readily verifi-
able outcomes (Gunjal et al., 2026; Viswanathan et al., 2026). Rubrics as Rewards uses expert-
guided, instance-specific rubrics as on-policy RL rewards, exploring both explicit aggregation of
criterion scores and implicit aggregation by an LLM grader (Gunjal et al., 2026). Reinforcement
Learning from Checklist Feedback (RLCF) evaluates instruction-specific requirements using LLM
graders and specialized verification programs, and converts aggregated checklist scores into prefer-
ence pairs for direct preference optimization (Viswanathan et al., 2026). RubricHub scales rubric su-
pervision through coarse-to-fine generation that combines principle-guided synthesis, multi-model
aggregation, and difficulty evolution, supporting rubric-based RL (Li et al., 2026). These methods
establish rubrics as a practical interface for specifying training objectives. We build on this paradigm
while distinguishing coverage of specified requirements from the quality of all generated content.

Rubric Specification and Reward Hacking.  Rubric-based optimization remains vulnerable to
incomplete reward specifications. Mahmoud et al. (2026) distinguish verifier failure from rubric-
design limitations: even with strong verification, rubric-based judges can prefer RL-trained re-
sponses that rubric-free judges rate worse overall, with improved completeness but degraded fac-
tual correctness and necessity.  Several approaches address insufficiently specified requirements.
RLCF adds universal criteria against excessive or off-topic content after observing verbose pream-
bles under checklist optimization (Viswanathan et al., 2026). Zhang et al. (2026) refine rubrics
by contrasting high-quality, diverse responses, improving discrimination in the high-reward region
and delaying over-optimization. OnlineRubrics augments existing criteria through comparisons be-
tween current-policy and control-policy responses (Rezaei et al., 2025). DR Tulu maintains search-
grounded, evolving rubrics that capture both newly discovered useful content and undesirable be-
haviors observed in rollouts (Shao et al., 2025). AMARIS further uses persistent training diagnostics
to revise criteria and weights in response to recurring failures (Wu et al., 2026). These specification-
refinement strategies motivate a complementary question: how should generated content be eval-
uated when coverage requirements remain incomplete? Our method supplements rubric coverage
with unit-level assessments of necessity and factual correctness, rather than relying on criterion
expansion alone, and does not require online rubric updates.

Fine-Grained Evaluation and Precision–Recall Rewards.  Content-level evaluation provides a
complementary perspective on response quality. Min et al. (2023) measure factual precision as
the proportion of generated atomic facts supported by a reliable knowledge source. Song et al.
(2024) combine sentence-level faithfulness assessment with keyfact–sentence alignment to measure
completeness and conciseness, distinguishing coverage of target information from the fraction of
generated sentences contributing that information. Related work directly incorporates precision–
recall signals into policy optimization.  Jhaveri et al. (2025) train clinical note generators with a
claim-level F1 reward combining entailment-based precision against the source dialogue and recall
over dialogue-derived claims. For video captioning, Gao et al. (2026) verify atomic claims against
video evidence and balance factual precision with reference-claim coverage, assigning attenuated
credit to supported but unmatched details and bounding their contribution to recall. Our work adapts
this content-level perspective to rubric-based RL: necessity is derived from a unit’s contribution to
prompt-specific coverage requirements, and precision counts units that satisfy both necessity and
factual correctness. Combining this precision with rubric-coverage recall through an Fβ reward
couples what a response should cover with whether its generated content is warranted and correct.

3  FORK: PRECISION-AWARE RUBRIC REWARD

In this section, we propose FORK, which can mitigate reward hacking in rubric-based RL by incorpo-
rating a precision objective (Figure 2). We first provide background on the standard rubric-coverage
reward formulation (Section 3.1), describe how to measure the precision of model responses (Sec-
tion 3.2), and introduce FORK as a new reward formulation for rubric-based RL (Section 3.3).

3.2  MEASURING PRECISION OF FREE-FORM RESPONSES

Response Unitization.  We first transform the response into an ordered sequence of units:
                               U(y) = (u1, . . . , un).
For simplicity, we define a unit as approximately a single sentence in the original response4. We
preserve the original wording and substantive content, making only minimal substitutions needed to
make each unit self-contained. For example, a pronoun may be replaced with its antecedent while
retaining the remainder of the original sentence. Purely functional or organizational material—such
as greetings, section headers, discourse markers (e.g., “first” or “in summary”), and transition-only
sentences or phrases—is discarded.  Unitization therefore isolates the substantive content of the
response without summarizing or rewriting it. We automate this process using an LLM unitizer5.

Credit-based Necessity.  We determine whether each unit contributes necessary content toward
the coverage rubrics. For every rubric–unit pair (rj, ui), an LLM grader predicts
                   mji = I[ui contributes to addressing rj] ∈{0, 1}.
Let Kj = Pni=1 mji be the number of units matched to rubric rj. We assign each rubric a total
unit-credit budget of one and divide this credit equally among its matched units. The credit assigned

4  EXPERIMENTS

Our experiments address four research questions: (RQ1) Can FORK improve response precision
and overall quality while maintaining comparable rubric coverage? (RQ2) Do these improvements
generalize to external benchmarks? (RQ3) How do precision estimation and precision–recall aggre-
gation affect performance? (RQ4) Are the observed improvements robust to changes in evaluation
graders and judge prompts?

4.1  SETUP

Base Model and Datasets.  We mainly use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025) as the base
policy model, following previous rubric-based RL work (Gunjal et al., 2026; Rezaei et al., 2025;
Mahmoud et al., 2026). We separately train the model on two subsets of RubricHub (Li et al.,
2026), Medical and Science. Each example consists of a prompt (or query) x sourced from existing
open-ended QA datasets and a set of evaluation rubrics and their weights R(x) synthesized through
multiple steps using LLMs. In our experiments, for the Science subset, we exclude examples sourced
from ResearchQA (Yifei et al., 2026) to use this benchmark as a separate evaluation suite. As
a result, the Medical subset contains 12,519 training examples and 1,391 test examples, and the
Science subset contains 19,806 training examples and 2,201 test examples.

Baselines and Ablations.  Based on their reward formulations, we categorize other methods de-
signed to mitigate reward hacking in rubric-based RL into the following three types:

(1) Rubric-Coverage Rewards include OnlineRubrics (Rezaei et al., 2025), which adds comple-
mentary rubrics to predefined rubrics at every training step by comparing current-policy and control-
policy responses; Universal Rubrics6, which adds two generic criteria (necessity and factual correct-
ness) to the predefined rubrics; and Reweighted Absence-Based Rubrics, which increases the weights
wj of absence-based rubrics. We also include the Standard rubric-coverage reward (Gunjal et al.,
2026) for reference.
(2) Preference-Based Rewards include Holistic Preference7, which assigns rewards according to an
LLM grader’s overall preference based on rubric coverage, necessity, and factual correctness when
comparing current-policy and control-policy responses. If a current-policy response is preferred, the
reward is 1; if it is less preferred, the reward is 0; and if the responses are tied, the reward is 0.5.

(3) Ablations: FORK Rewards with Precision Variants include Generic Criteria, which measures
precision as the fraction of necessary and factually correct units, both determined by prompting an
LLM grader; Offline Criteria, which elicits prompt-specific precision criteria by comparing different
model responses before training; and Online Criteria, which elicits precision criteria specific to
current-policy responses at each training step.

For simplicity, we use base policy responses as control-policy responses. Implementation details
for the baselines and ablations we introduce are provided in Appendix A, and their corresponding
training prompts are provided in Appendix E.1.


Training Grader Selection.  We select an LLM to use as the training grader for all rubric-based
RL experiments. To do that, we first select five candidate training graders and measure agreements of
their judgments with three frontier models. The result shows that Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (Qwen Team,
2026b) shows the strongest correlation (r = 0.714), so we use this model as a universal training
grader. Please refer to Appendix B for the detailed grader selection process.


Evaluation.  We evaluate in-domain performance on RubricHub test sets and cross-dataset gen-
eralization on external benchmarks (i.e., HealthBench (Arora et al., 2025) for the medical domain
and ResearchQA (Yifei et al., 2026) for the science domain). We conduct two types of evaluations:
(1) rubric-based evaluations measuring macro rubric recall, macro precision, and macro F1, and
(2) rubric-free evaluations reporting the overall win rate against the base policy model and average
response length (i.e., verbosity). We use GPT-5.4 (Singh et al., 2025), a strong frontier LLM, as a
reference grader for automating these evaluations. We use the same rubric-free judge prompt as in
Mahmoud et al. (2026) and provide newly introduced judge prompts in Appendix E.2. To measure
precision, a reference grader directly determines unit necessity and factual correctness given a set of
coverage rubrics. We compute the F1 score as the harmonic mean of rubric recall and precision.


Hyperparameters and Implementation Details.  We update the policy model using the group
relative policy optimization (GRPO) algorithm (Shao et al., 2024) with 16 rollouts per prompt, a
context length of 2,000 tokens, and a sampling temperature of 1.0. Models are trained with a batch
size of 128, a learning rate of 4.2 × 10−6, and a constant schedule with 5% linear warmup for 3
epochs on 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs.

4.3  ANALYSIS

Improvement in satisfaction of both presence- and absence-based rubrics.  In RubricHub,
most of the coverage reward weight falls on presence-based rubrics rather than absence-based
rubrics (Mahmoud et al., 2026). Accordingly, we observe that the standard coverage reward sig-
nificantly increases the presence-based rubric satisfaction rate (+15.0 pp) while slightly decreasing
the absence-based rubric satisfaction rate (-3.2 pp) (Table 3). This implies that the model learns
to add both desirable and undesirable content. Rubric-coverage reward baselines produce more de-
sirable content but fail to suppress undesirable content.  In contrast, our method nearly matches
the presence-based rubric satisfaction rate of the standard coverage reward while improving the
absence-based rubric satisfaction rate (+5.7 pp).

Robustness to rubric-free evaluation judge prompts. Since rubric-free win rates depend heavily
on LLM judge prompt instructions, we verify whether our method is robust to prompt variations. To
do so, we adopt another judge prompt from Rezaei et al. (2025) and verify on the medical domain
(Table 4). Since rubric-coverage reward baselines have significantly degraded win rates, we do not
include those results. Our method mostly outperforms the other baselines on both evaluation suites,
supporting its robustness to different judge prompts.

Isolating the effectiveness of our reward formulation. We use a reference grader for our main
evaluation (Section 4.2). However, this evaluation combines the effect of our reward formulation
with the effect of transferring to a stronger, unseen grader. To isolate the former, we use the training
grader for evaluation (Figure 3). The results show that FORK rewards have the highest win rates
among rubric-based RL methods, while β = 1.25 shows Pareto-optimal performance that balances
rubric coverage and overall win rate.  This supports the effectiveness of our reward formulation
compared to the baselines.

Different precision–recall aggregations. To justify using a harmonic mean to aggregate precision
and recall, we compare F1 score aggregation with simple averaging (Table 6). The results show
that simple averaging yields slightly higher precision (+2.1 pp) but lower recall (-6.3 pp) than F1
score aggregation. We observe that higher precision compensates for lower rubric recall during
training. We also investigate whether further increasing the weight of recall in Fβ score aggregation
is beneficial. To do so, we train a model with F2 score aggregation but observe a detrimental effect
on both precision and recall. We leave finding the optimal value of β for future work.

Our unit necessity prediction outperforms prompting-based method. To measure the accuracy
of unit necessity prediction, we progressively remove units predicted as unnecessary from a re-
sponse and measure how much rubric recall score is decreased (Figure 4). Specifically, we randomly
sample 40 base model responses from RubricHub-Medical containing 723 units and progressively
remove units starting from unnecessary ones and then necessary ones once all unnecessary units

5  CONCLUSION

In this work, we introduce FORK, a simple method that mitigates reward hacking in rubric-based RL,
by adding a complementary precision objective to the original recall-based reward. To address the
observed reward hacking behavior, we define a precision objective to require each unit of a model
response to be necessary and factually correct. We propose rubric-driven necessity credits to faith-
fully measure necessity and use an LLM grader to measure factual correctness. Our experimental
results show that FORK outperforms other rubric-based RL methods and achieves Pareto-optimal
performance that balances recall and overall response quality.

A  DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF OTHER RUBRIC-BASED RL METHODS

In Section 4.1, we briefly describe which baselines and ablations we adopt. Since there are newly
introduced methods, in this section, we further describe how those are implemented in detail.


A.1  RUBRIC-COVERAGE REWARDS

Universal Rubrics adds two generic rubrics corresponds to necessity and factual correctness to a pre-
defined set of rubrics. The verbatim rubrics are: (1) “The model response should be necessary: every
part is needed, with nothing repeated, padded, tangential, or included only to sound thorough.”, (2)
“The model response should be factually correct: every claim is correct and supported, with nothing
fabricated, misleading, or overconfident.”.

Reweighted Absence-Based Rubrics first determines absence-based rubrics.  If a rubric contains
both presence-based component and absence-based component, we consider it as an absence-based.
Then, given these absence-based rubrics and their original weights, an LLM increases their weights.
The prompt is provided in Figure 10–11.


A.2  PREFERENCE-BASED REWARDS

Holistic Preference let an LLM grader to decide its preference of current policy responses against
base policy responses and uses as a reward. The prompt is provided in Figure 12–13.


A.3  ABLATIONS: FORK REWARDS WITH PRECISION VARIANTS

These rewards use the same structure of reward as our method with F1 aggregation, but use different
methods of measuring precision.

Generic Criteria replaces our credit-based necessity prediction to a simple prompting method.
Therefore, an LLM grader is given a set of response units and corresponding rubrics and determines
each unit’s necessity and factual correctness, respectively. The prompt is provided in Figure 14.

Offline Criteria generates prompt-specific precision criteria and use those for measuring precision,
instead of using two generic criteria, necessity and factual correctness. To generate those criteria, an
LLM grader is given two responses, one from a base policy model and another from a RL-trained
policy model with standard coverage reward, and then generates precision criteria by comparing
those two responses. During online RL training, these precision criteria are used for every training
step. The prompt is provided in Figure 15–16.

Online Criteria also generates prompt-specific precision criteria but updates those at every training
step. To do that, an LLM grader is given a current policy response and a set of rubrics, and gen-
erates online precision criteria by evaluating the response quality. During online RL training, these
precision criteria are updated for every training step. The prompt is provided in Figure 17–18.

D.1  MEDICAL: A CONSUMER HEALTH QUESTION


  Prompt


  Do allergies cause coughing? I had a bad cough all winter, and it went away for a couple months when the weather got warmer. It just
    started again since the weather has gotten very hot (90 degrees) and rainy. Is there a cough medicine for coughs due to allergies?


  FORK (ours)


   Yes, allergies can indeed cause coughing, and your symptoms align with this possibility. Allergies can trigger a cough through several
   mechanisms, including:
    1.  Postnasal Drip: When allergens like pollen, dust mites, or mold irritate the nasal passages, they can cause increased mucus produc-
        tion. This excess mucus can drip down the back of the throat (postnasal drip), stimulating the cough reflex, especially at night or
     when lying down.
    2.  Inflammation and Irritation: Allergic reactions can cause inflammation in the airways, which can lead to a dry, hacking cough,
       especially if the airways are sensitive to the allergens.
    3. Bronchospasm: In some cases, allergies can cause the airways to narrow, leading to wheezing and coughing, which is more common
       in individuals with asthma that is triggered by allergies.
   The pattern you described—coughing worsening in the winter and returning with hot, rainy weather—suggests that your cough may be
   related to seasonal allergies, possibly to mold, pollen, or other outdoor allergens that are more prevalent in these conditions, or to indoor
   allergens that may be more active in warmer, humid weather (like dust mites, mold, or pet dander).

  Cough Medicines for Allergy-Induced Cough
   There are several types of cough medicines that can help manage allergy-induced cough, but it’s important to note that they should be
   used under the guidance of a healthcare provider, especially if you have underlying health conditions or are taking other medications.
   Here are some options:
    1.  Antihistamines: These can help reduce the symptoms of allergies, including sneezing, itching, and runny nose, which can contribute
       to coughing. Common over-the-counter antihistamines include diphenhydramine (Benadryl) and cetirizine (Zyrtec). However, they
      can cause drowsiness, so it’s best to use them before bedtime or to avoid activities that require alertness, like driving.
    2.  Decongestants: These can help relieve nasal congestion, which can contribute to postnasal drip and cough, but they do not directly
        treat the cough itself. Pseudoephedrine (Sudafed) is a common decongestant, but it can have side effects like increased heart rate and
      blood pressure, so it’s not recommended for people with high blood pressure, heart disease, or prostate issues.
    3. Cough Suppressants: These are used to suppress a cough that is dry and unproductive, and they include dextromethorphan (found
       in many cough syrups and cold medicines). However, if your cough is productive (producing phlegm), a cough suppressant may not
      be the best choice, as it can prevent you from clearing the mucus that your body is trying to expel.
    4.  Expectorants: These help loosen mucus and make it easier to cough up, and they include guaifenesin (found in Mucinex). They
      can be helpful if your cough is productive, but they do not address the underlying allergy.
    5.  Nasal Corticosteroids: These are often more effective for long-term allergy management and can help reduce inflammation in the
       nasal passages, which can contribute to coughing. Examples include fluticasone (Flonase) and mometasone (Nasonex), which are
       available by prescription.

   Recommendations
   Given the pattern of your cough, it might be beneficial to use a combination of an antihistamine and a decongestant, or an antihistamine
   and an expectorant, to manage your symptoms. However, since you’ve had a persistent cough for an extended period, it’s important to
   consult a healthcare provider to rule out other potential causes, such as a respiratory infection, asthma, or acid reflux, which can also
   cause chronic cough.
   Here are some steps you can take:
    1.  Identify Triggers: Keep track of when your cough occurs (e.g., during certain times of the day, after being outside, or when the
      weather changes) to help identify potential allergens.
    2.  Environmental Control: Use air conditioning to keep indoor humidity low (below 50%), use dehumidifiers, and regularly clean or
       replace air filters to reduce mold and dust mites.
    3.  Allergy Testing: Consider getting allergy testing to identify specific triggers, which can help in tailoring your treatment plan,
      whether it’s through environmental control, allergy shots, or targeted medications.
    4.  Consult a Healthcare Provider: Speak with a healthcare provider, such as an allergist or primary care physician, to discuss
      your symptoms, undergo a physical examination, and possibly undergo allergy testing, and to get a prescription for appropriate
       medications, which might include a combination of antihistamines, decongestants, and/or nasal corticosteroids, as well as advice on
      managing your cough, such as using a humidifier, staying hydrated, and avoiding irritants like cigarette smoke and air pollution.
    If your cough is severe, accompanied by shortness of breath, wheezing, fever, or blood in your phlegm, or if it persists for more than 8
   weeks, it’s crucial to seek medical attention, as these symptoms could indicate a more serious condition that requires specific treatment,

  is still “presence”.
  - The “updated weight” field should be an integer for an absence-based item, chosen as described un-
  der Updated weights below, and null for a presence-based item.

 # Criterion types
 Each rubric item is presence-based or absence-based. The type is a property of the item’s wording,
  read before looking at the response:  it does not depend on the response or on whether the item is met.
  - A presence-based item asks whether the response contains something: a fact, a reasoning step, a list,
  a recommendation, a disclaimer, a format or a tone.  It is met when that content appears explicitly in
  the response and not met when the content is missing. Missing content makes the item not met; it does
  not make the item absence-based.  If your explanation says that the response does not mention, state or
  provide what the item asks for, the item is presence-based.
  - An absence-based item asks whether the response avoids something.  Its wording forbids or limits:
   it does not claim, recommend, include, assume or fabricate X; it contains no errors or contradictions;
   it stays within a limit such as “only X” or “without Y”.  It is met only if nothing anywhere in the re-
  sponse violates it, so check the whole response before marking it met, and say in the explanation what
 you checked for and what you found.
  - When a rubric item has both a presence requirement and an absence requirement, label it “absence”,
  even when most of the item asks for content and the prohibition or limit is a single clause.  Its “crite-
  ria met” still requires both parts to be met.
  - A correctness word attached to a specific required fact, such as “correctly states that X”, makes a
  presence-based item, not an absence-based one.

 # Updated weights
 Each rubric item is listed with its weight. Rubrics like this one describe what a good answer should
  contain in many items but what it must avoid in only a few, because the ways an answer can go wrong
  cannot be enumerated the way its required content can. Graded as listed, such a rubric rewards adding
  content: every extra sentence might satisfy one more presence-based item, while the few absence-based
  items cost little to violate, so answers grow padded and error-prone. The absence-based items are the
  rubric’s only guards against that, and their listed weights understate that role. Beside deciding whether
  each item is met, your second job is to state, for each absence-based item, the weight it should carry,
  so that the grade favors an answer that says what is needed, says it correctly, and stops there.

 Reweighted Absence-Based Rubrics (2/2)

  Size the weight against the presence-based items the item guards: violating it should cost at least as

  eral times over. A narrowly worded item could deserve as much when the detail it protects is decisive,

  and as high as the case warrants. The response’s own statements about what it avoids do not raise the
  weight. For presence-based items, “updated weight” is null.

  Online Criteria (1/2)

 ===SYSTEM===
 You are an expert medical evaluator. You are given a user QUESTION and a model’s ANSWER to it,
  split into numbered UNITS — exact spans of the answer (sentences, list items, clauses), in its order,
  under its [section] lines. The answer was graded against a coverage rubric — the points a good an-
  swer should contain — and each point it earned was credited to the units carrying it: those are tagged
  (credited), the rest (uncredited). Credited units are the core content. Uncredited units are either periph-
  eral content the answer still needs — lead-ins, qualifiers, examples and details that extend a credited
  point, relevant points the rubric did not ask for — or futile content — padding, repetition, digression
 — and the tag does not say which: an uncredited unit may or may not have an undesirable property,
  and telling the two apart is where your job starts.

 Your task is to write the PRECISION RUBRIC for this answer: extract the undesirable properties the
  uncredited units show, if any, and turn each into a criterion. A tag records information — which point
  a unit carries; a property is behaviour — what a unit does toward the question, the units around it or
  its own point. A property is undesirable when deleting units that have it leaves the answer no worse
  for the asker: no less correct, complete or clear. For example: saying again what an earlier unit al-
  ready said (adding a detail, example, qualifier or reason to a point is not saying it again); filler, pleas-
  antries, sign-offs or commentary on the answer itself; generic advice that would fit any question on the
  topic; explaining or justifying at length where a unit of that kind only needs to name the point; drifting
  to a topic the question did not ask about.  It helps to ask what an uncredited unit does that the credited
  units need not do — but a tag is not a property: credited units can show these properties too, to some
  degree, and uncredited units can carry something the asker needs — a qualifier, an example, a point of
  their own. A separate grader will apply every criterion to every unit, credited or not, without seeing the
  tags, your analysis or your unit lists; a unit is good only if it satisfies every criterion that applies to it.

  Rules:
  - Each criterion is a scope and a prohibition. “scope” is “units that <kind of content >” — a kind of
  content, never a section, a position or one particular unit. “criterion” begins “A unit should not . . . ”
  and is one prohibition the grader can check on the unit alone; only a repeat may refer to earlier units.
 Make the scope “all units” when the property is not confined to one kind of unit but should be checked
 on every unit of the answer.
  - A criterion is about what a unit does, never about the point it makes or fails to make — what the
  answer must contain is judged by the coverage rubric, not by your criteria.  If a credited unit shows
  the property because of the point it makes, the criterion is about content: narrow the scope or drop it.
  Content the question asks for is never off-topic, even when uncredited.
  - Write a criterion only for an undesirable property you actually see in the uncredited units, and list the
  uncredited units that show it (uncredited only). Write as many criteria as there are distinct undesirable
  properties; an uncredited unit showing no such property needs no criterion, and if none does, return an
 empty list.
  - One property per criterion. Prefer several simple criteria to one that chains different properties with
  “or”; “or” is fine between forms of the same property (pleasantries or sign-offs). Overlap between cri-
  teria is harmless; the same property twice is not.
  - The grader cannot see which units are credited. A criterion must never mention or depend on credit
 — the words credit, credited, uncredited and rubric do not appear in a scope or criterion. A repeat is
  judged against earlier units of any kind: write “an earlier unit”, never “a credited unit”.
  - Do not quote, paraphrase, number or otherwise point at units in “scope” or “criterion”: each must
 make sense applied to any unit of any answer to this question.
  - Begin with a brief “analysis” (a few sentences): which uncredited units the answer needs and which
 show an undesirable property — by groups, not one by one — and what the latter do that the answer
 would be no worse without. Then write the criteria.
