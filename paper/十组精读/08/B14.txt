                                     ABSTRACT

                          Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) improves reasoning in
                          language models, but scalar outcomes provide coarse supervision over response
                                 trajectories. Self-distillation offers denser token-level guidance by comparing an2026                        unassisted policy with a feedback-informed self-teacher, yet the teacher evolves
                          with the student, creating unstable targets and motivating additional regulariza-
                                 tion. We introduce Function-Space Policy Optimization (FSPO), a single-policyOct                     framework that combines current privileged adaptation with function-space tempo-
                                  ral consolidation. FSPO stores sparse historical next-token distributions as fixed
                           temporal targets, providing temporal separation without maintaining a persistent14
                              teacher model. It further introduces privileged disagreement correction to explicitly
                            suppress locally overvalued actions, reliability-adaptive grounding that strengthens
                             reference supervision when self-generated evidence repeatedly fails, and quality-
                         and coverage-aware temporal memory that retains verified behaviors and improves
                             privileged demonstrations. Across reasoning and tool-use benchmarks, FSPO
                            achieves the highest average accuracy under a 5-hour training budget, improving[cs.LG]                       over the strongest baseline by approximately 4% points on average while reducing
                         peak memory from about 70 GB to 56 GB on Olmo3-7B-Instruct.

changes whenever the policy parameters are updated. Although stop-gradient prevents gradient
flow through the teacher within an optimization step, it does not provide temporal separation across
training iterations, creating a moving-target problem that can weaken the stability of the distillation
signal. SDPO therefore relies on stabilization mechanisms such as EMA or trust-region regularization;
the EMA variant requires an additional persistent parameter copy, introducing memory overhead that
grows with model size. SDPO also treats student–teacher disagreement primarily through distribution
matching and does not explicitly distinguish ordinary mismatch from cases where the student strongly
overvalues a candidate rejected by the privileged view. Such severe local disagreements may require
more targeted directional correction. In addition, SDPO lacks a mechanism for adapting supervision
according to the reliability of the policy’s behavior on individual problems. Examples that the policy
repeatedly fails to solve are therefore not explicitly differentiated, in supervision strength, from those
for which reliable self-generated guidance is consistently available. Finally, SDPO primarily relies
on the current feedback-conditioned policy and does not maintain an explicit temporal memory of
useful privileged predictions discovered earlier in training. As a result, informative past behaviors are
not systematically preserved and replayed, nor are historical experiences selected to balance solution
quality with structural diversity. These limitations restrict stable temporal supervision, targeted local
correction, reliability-aware learning, and preservation of complementary behaviors across training.

 Can we achieve temporally stable and reliability-aware self-distillation without maintaining a
   separate teacher model, while preserving useful knowledge from earlier stages of training?

FSPO addresses these limitations through a unified self-distillation framework that improves temporal
stability, strengthens local correction, adapts supervision to policy reliability, and preserves useful
historical knowledge. To mitigate the moving-target problem, informative privileged predictions from
earlier optimization steps are materialized as sparse next-token distributions and retained as fixed
temporal targets. Because these historical function values remain unchanged while the current policy
evolves, they provide temporal separation across training iterations without requiring a persistent
EMA teacher or an additional resident parameter copy. FSPO also goes beyond distribution-level
matching by identifying candidates that the unassisted policy strongly prefers but the privileged
view rejects, and explicitly suppressing these locally overvalued decisions. This distinguishes severe
privileged disagreement from ordinary distribution mismatch and provides a more targeted directional
learning signal. Supervision is further adapted according to the reliability of the policy on each
problem over time. A smoothed estimate of rollout performance distinguishes problems on which the
policy repeatedly fails from those for which it consistently produces reliable solutions. When the
policy repeatedly fails and its own evidence becomes unreliable, the reference loss is strengthened; as
the policy improves and begins to solve the problem consistently, this supervision is progressively
reduced. FSPO additionally maintains a temporal memory of useful privileged behaviors discovered
earlier in training. This memory provides fixed historical targets for consolidation and stronger
demonstrations when the current rollout group lacks a verified successful response. By retaining
trajectories according to both solution quality and structural complementarity, FSPO preserves diverse,
nonredundant solution strategies and enables the policy to benefit from complementary historical
knowledge throughout optimization. As shown in Figure 1, these design choices translate into the
highest average accuracy across the five tasks while requiring the fewest generated tokens. Our
contributions are summarized as follows:

• Preserves temporal knowledge through sparse historical next-token distributions while combining
  current privileged adaptation with historical function-space consolidation, enabling temporally
  separated supervision with a single resident policy.

• Introduces targeted privileged disagreement correction that suppresses candidates strongly over-
  valued by the unassisted policy but rejected by the privileged view, providing stronger directional
   correction beyond distribution matching.

• Adapts reference supervision to the reliability of self-generated guidance, strengthening it when the
  policy repeatedly fails and reducing it as reliable solutions emerge. Quality- and coverage-aware
  temporal memory further preserves diverse, nonredundant behaviors.

• Achieves approximately 4% points higher average accuracy than the strongest baseline across
  reasoning and tool-use benchmarks, while reducing peak memory from about 70 GB to 56 GB for
  Olmo3-7B-Instruct by eliminating the persistent EMA teacher.

2  FUNCTION-SPACE POLICY OPTIMIZATION

Self-distillation provides dense supervision by comparing an unassisted policy with a privileged
self-teacher conditioned on stronger evidence. However, because both views evolve with the same
policy, the teacher remains a moving target across optimization steps. FTSD addresses this by
preserving informative historical policy outputs directly in function space rather than maintaining a
persistent parameter-space teacher. Let πθs denote the policy at step s, with
                                  Fθs(c) = πθs(· | x, y<t)                                    (1)

for prefix c = (x, y<t). Selected privileged predictions are materialized as sparse next-token distribu-
tions and retained as temporal targets, providing temporal separation as the policy evolves. FTSD
further combines current privileged adaptation with targeted disagreement correction for strongly
overvalued actions, quality- and coverage-aware temporal consolidation of complementary historical
behaviors, and reliability-adaptive reference grounding. Reference supervision is strengthened when
self-generated evidence fails and progressively reduced as reliable behavior emerges, yielding stable,
selective, and adaptive self-distillation within a single-policy framework. Figure 2 illustrates the
overall framework, while Algorithm 1 summarizes the complete training procedure.

2.1  PRIVILEGED ADAPTATION AND DISAGREEMENT CORRECTION

We first construct dense supervision by comparing the current policy under an unassisted view and
a privileged view with access to stronger evidence. Let D = {(xp, gp)}Np=1 denote the training set,
where gp is the verified reference solution. For each problem, the policy samples G responses,

                        yp,j ∼πθs(· | xp),      rp,j = R(xp, yp,j, gp) ∈[0, 1].                    (2)

An unsuccessful rollout reveals where the policy fails, but not how its local token decisions should
change. We therefore construct a privileged demonstration using the strongest reliable evidence
available:
            yp,k,  ∃k ̸= j : rp,k ≥τsucc,

This hierarchy, with success threshold τsucc > 0, prioritizes a verified sibling response, followed by
previously retained policy behavior, while using the reference solution as a reliable fallback. At each

            pp,j,t = πθs(· | xp, yp,j,<t),       qp,j,t = stopgrad [πθs(· | xp ⊕dp,j, yp,j,<t)] .       (4)

Since both views share the same policy and response history, their disagreement reflects how privi-
leged evidence changes the next-token prediction at the same response state.

2.2  FUNCTION-SPACE TEMPORAL CONSOLIDATION

Privileged adaptation provides supervision at the current optimization step, but its target evolves
together with the policy. Thus, stop-gradient separates the student and self-teacher within an update,
but does not provide temporal separation across updates. We address this by preserving selected
privileged predictions from earlier stages as fixed function-space targets rather than maintaining a
persistent historical teacher.

For each problem p, the temporal memory stores a retained trajectory ˜yp, its verifier score ˜rp,
structural signature σp, and sparse privileged predictions {˜Sp,t, ˜qp,t}t. To improve the usefulness of
this memory, trajectories are retained based on both verifier quality and structural complementarity
rather than reward alone. This discourages repeatedly storing similar solution patterns and promotes
broader coverage of distinct behaviors. A candidate replaces the stored trajectory when its retention
score, which combines verifier quality with the coverage of its structural signature, exceeds that of
the stored entry. During consolidation, memories are also sampled evenly across structural groups,
preventing dominant signatures from overwhelming replay.

˜rp ≥τsucc and ˜dp = gp otherwise. The source policy produces

Unlike the current privileged target, ˜qp,t remains fixed while the policy evolves. Privileged adaptation

To avoid stale targets without collapsing temporal separation, we use delayed and staggered refresh.
A stored target becomes eligible for refresh after H optimization steps, and a fraction ζ of eligible
entries is updated every F steps. This maintains temporal targets of different ages while allowing the
memory to gradually track policy improvement.

3.2  MAIN RESULTS

We first examine whether the proposed temporal self-distillation strategy translates into consistent
improvements across tasks, model backbones, and training budgets. Table 1 shows that FSPO achieves
the highest Avg@5h for both Qwen3-8B and Olmo3-7B-Instruct, with the best 5-hour performance
on every scientific reasoning task. The improvements remain consistent across Chemistry, Physics,
Biology, and Materials, suggesting that the gains are not tied to a particular domain or backbone. The
margin is smaller on Tool use, where the baselines already reach performance close to ours within
the same budget. Even in this setting, FSPO remains competitive at the 5-hour budget, matching the
strongest Qwen3-8B result and improving over the baselines on Olmo3-7B-Instruct, although SDPO
is stronger at the 1-hour budget on both backbones.

The same behavior becomes clearer when considering optimization over time. As shown in Figure 3,
FSPO obtains a large fraction of its final improvement early in training and approaches its peak
substantially faster than the baselines across most tasks. Figure 4 further shows that this faster learning
is accompanied by a rapid reduction in per-step cost.  Privileged supervision and disagreement
correction provide stronger local guidance by suppressing overvalued token choices, while temporal
consolidation reinforces previously successful solution patterns. As these signals accumulate, the
policy increasingly produces shorter responses, which contributes to its lower generation cost. In
contrast, GRPO and SDPO tend to generate longer trajectories as training progresses, causing their
generation-dominated step time to gradually increase.

3.5  ABLATION STUDY

We ablate the main components of FSPO on Olmo3-7B-Instruct across Biology and Tool Use to
assess their contributions. As shown in Table 2, we ablate the objective, privileged demonstrations,
temporal supervision, memory retention, and adaptive grounding while keeping all other settings
fixed. Together, these components raise average accuracy from 27.8% for the zero-shot base model to
64.5%, reflecting their complementary contributions. Additional analyses of the choices are provided
in Appendix A.3.

Effective supervision. Case 1 first reveals that FSPO benefits from combining complementary
forms of supervision rather than relying solely on privileged distillation. Temporal consolidation


                                       8

===== PDF PAGE 9 =====
and reference grounding account for the largest gains, particularly on Tool Use, while disagreement
correction provides a smaller but consistent improvement. Importantly, these signals depend on
one another: retaining disagreement correction after removing both temporal and reference anchors
performs worse than distillation alone, suggesting that aggressive local correction is useful only
when the evolving self-teacher is sufficiently stabilized. The source of privileged evidence is equally
important. Reference-only supervision leads to a substantial drop, whereas verified siblings and
retained trajectories remain closer to the policy’s response distribution. Their combination in the full
hierarchy therefore provides the strongest current supervision (Case 2).

Temporal stability. The remaining ablations show that temporal stability depends not simply on
retaining past information, but on what is preserved and how it is reused. Sparse function-space
targets outperform parameter snapshots, EMA teachers, frozen policies, and trajectory-level CE
replay; several of these alternatives even fall below removing temporal consolidation altogether
(Case 3). Within the memory itself, retaining trajectories by both quality and coverage performs
better than either criterion alone, while uniform or random replay further weakens the historical
signal (Case 4). This selective preservation is complemented by reliability-adaptive grounding, which
improves over constant or binary reference weighting by adjusting supervision as the policy becomes
more reliable (Case 5). Finally, cyclic adaptation followed by consolidation performs best, whereas
reversing this order causes a marked degradation, supporting the intended progression from acquiring
new privileged information to stabilizing it over time (Case 6).



4  RELATED WORK


Policy-gradient methods (Williams, 2004; Schulman et al., 2017) underpin modern RLVR, with
GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and its variants (Yu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025) improving reasoning,
coding, tool use, and visual grounding (Team et al., 2025; Lambert et al., 2024; El-Kishky et al.,
2025; Khatri et al., 2025; Karim & Verma, 2026). However, trajectory-level rewards provide coarse
credit when feedback is sparse or uniform across rollouts (Kazemnejad et al., 2024; Chen et al.,
2025b). Prior work addresses this through step-level verifiers (Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al.,
2024; Setlur et al., 2024), dense reward redistribution (Cui et al., 2025; Chan et al., 2024; Cao et al.,
2025b), and selective updates at high-entropy decisions (Wang et al., 2025). Richer supervision
has also emerged through critiques (Scheurer et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2025; Song et al., 2026), self-
reflection (Shinn et al., 2023; Madaan et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2024), hindsight (Andrychowicz et al.,
2017; Liu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023), context distillation (Snell et al., 2022; Choi et al., 2022;
Kujanpää et al., 2024), and verified self-training (Zelikman et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024). On-policy
self-distillation brings these ideas together by conditioning the same policy on references (Zhao et al.,
2026), demonstrations (Shenfeld et al., 2026), or feedback and successful siblings (Hubotter et al.,
2026), and SALD (Das et al., 2026c) extends a similar self-referenced objective, akin to SDPO and
SDFT, to text-to-image diffusion models (Das et al., 2026a;b; 2025); yet the resulting teacher evolves
with the student and can suppress useful uncertainty (Kim et al., 2026; Das et al., 2026d). FTSD
retains this single-policy setting while introducing temporally fixed historical supervision.

Knowledge distillation transfers teacher distributions to a student (Hinton et al., 2015; Romero et al.,
2014), with sequence-level and on-policy variants extending it to language models (Kim & Rush,
2016; Gu et al., 2023; Agarwal et al., 2023). Student-generated trajectories reduce train–inference
mismatch (Ross et al., 2010), while divergence choice governs how teacher behavior is matched
(Amini et al., 2025). Evolving targets are commonly stabilized through target networks (Mnih
et al., 2015), EMA teachers (Grill et al., 2020; Tarvainen & Valpola, 2017), trust regions (Schulman
et al., 2015), or expert iteration (Anthony et al., 2017). In parallel, on-policy adaptation can reduce
forgetting (Shenfeld et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025a), while continual learning relies on parameter
regularization (Kirkpatrick et al., 2016), output distillation (Li & Hoiem, 2016), and experience
replay (Rolnick et al., 2018). Closest to FTSD are function-space approaches that preserve previous
outputs (Benjamin & Kording, 2018; Pan et al., 2020), including stored-logit replay (Buzzega et al.,
2020). FTSD adapts this principle to LLM self-distillation through sparse privileged distributions
used only during training, rather than memory-augmented inference (Behrouz et al., 2024; Cao et al.,
2025a), learned context representations (Eyuboglu et al., 2025), or persistent model copies common
in RL systems (Sheng et al., 2024).

5  CONCLUSION

We introduced FSPO, a single-policy self-distillation framework that preserves historical behavior
via sparse function-space targets rather than a persistent teacher model. By combining privileged
adaptation with disagreement correction, reliability-adaptive grounding, and quality- and coverage-
aware temporal memory, FSPO provides stable supervision while retaining verified behaviors across
training. Across scientific reasoning and tool-use tasks on two model backbones, FSPO achieves the
highest average accuracy under a 5-hour wall-clock budget, converges faster, and better preserves
prior capabilities, while reducing estimated peak memory from about 70 GB to 56 GB on Olmo3-
7B-Instruct. The ablations further show that these gains arise from the interaction between current
adaptation, temporal consolidation, and adaptive grounding rather than any single component. Overall,
the results suggest that temporal knowledge can be preserved effectively in function space, enabling
more memory-efficient and stable self-distillation for continual post-training.

A.1  COMPLETE TRAINING PROCEDURE

Algorithm 2 expands Algorithm 1 into the complete training loop of FSPO, using the notation
of Section 2. Each step follows six stages: the policy explores the problem, learns from current
privileged evidence, is grounded on the reference solution when it remains unreliable, consolidates
fixed historical targets, updates its parameters, and finally refreshes its temporal memory. For
readability, mean denotes the average over valid response positions, matching the normalizers Zcur,
Ztemp, and ZCE, and Dα denotes the same generalized JSD (α = 0.5) used in Ldistill.

Each memory entry holds a retained trajectory ˜yp, its verifier score ˜rp, its structural signature σp
(the invoked action sequence for Tool Use, the predicted option for multiple-choice tasks), and its
sparse targets { ˜Sp,t, ˜qp,t}t. Memory is seeded with reference solutions for the problems in the initial
batches, marked with ˜rp = 0 so that they serve only as fallback targets and are not used as verified
demonstrations. Verified rollouts are added for problems not yet in memory and replace an existing
entry when their retention score, which combines verifier quality with signature coverage using a
coverage weight of 0.5, exceeds that of the stored trajectory.

A.2  ADDITIONAL DETAILS ON CATASTROPHIC FORGETTING

Table 3 provides a broader view of how task-specific adaptation affects capabilities already present in
the base model. Across all five adaptation datasets, standard GRPO produces the largest degradation
on the retained benchmarks, indicating substantial drift toward the newly optimized task. On-policy
GRPO reduces this degradation, while SDPO further improves retention through dense self-distillation.
FSPO consistently provides the strongest overall retention among the adapted models, achieving
the highest average score for every training dataset. On the scientific tasks, its average performance
remains close to or exceeds the original Olmo3-7B-Instruct checkpoint: Chemistry and Biology reach
60.95 and 60.94 compared with the base score of 61.26, while Physics and Materials improve to
62.61 and 62.32, respectively. These results indicate that preserving historical behavior in function
space can limit catastrophic forgetting without preventing beneficial transfer from the newly learned
task.

The benchmark-wise results further reveal how this behavior varies across prior capabilities. Hu-
manEval shows the clearest positive transfer under scientific adaptation with FSPO, increasing from
47.56 in the base model to 51.83–58.54 across Chemistry, Physics, Biology, and Materials, with the
largest improvement observed after Physics training. MMLU is comparatively stable, remaining close
to the original 58.77 and slightly exceeding it after Materials and Tool-use adaptation. In contrast,
HellaSwag, Winogrande, and TruthfulQA generally remain below their respective base-model scores,
indicating that these capabilities are more susceptible to task-specific adaptation. Tool-use training
represents the most challenging setting, where FSPO attains an average retention score of 59.45
compared with 61.26 for the base model. Nevertheless, it still outperforms GRPO (54.79), on-policy
GRPO (57.58), and SDPO (58.73) in overall retention. Taken together, these results show that
function-space temporal consolidation substantially reduces destructive behavioral drift while still
allowing improvements learned during adaptation to transfer to compatible prior capabilities.

A.5  TRAINING DYNAMICS

Final performance alone does not reveal how the components of FSPO interact during optimization.
We therefore examine the training dynamics in Figure 7, following how supervision evolves as the
policy moves from unreliable early rollouts toward stable self-generated guidance. The analysis
tracks how the strength and source of supervision change over time, how temporal disagreement is
preserved, how the cache matures, and how these mechanisms translate into improved rollout quality
and more stable generations. Figure 8 then complements this view by showing that the historical
memory supporting temporal consolidation remains negligible in cost even as the cache grows.

Figure 7(a) first shows how the effective loss budget changes over this transition. Reference grounding
contributes strongly at the beginning, when rollout quality is still low and the adaptive weight w(s)p
therefore places greater emphasis on LCE. Its contribution then falls rapidly as the policy begins to
produce reliable evidence on its own. The current privileged objective follows a similar decline as the
student aligns with its contemporaneous self-teacher. Temporal consolidation, in contrast, remains
active for longer because cached targets were produced by earlier policy states and therefore continue
to disagree with the evolving student. The smaller recurrent fluctuations later in training reflect the
cyclic ρs schedule, which repeatedly rebalances adaptation and consolidation rather than fixing their
relative importance throughout optimization.

The source of this privileged guidance evolves accordingly in Figure 7(b). Verified siblings already
account for a substantial portion of guidance early in training and soon become dominant as successful
rollouts become more frequent. Reference answers serve mainly as a fallback during the least reliable
stage, but their use quickly declines once the policy can generate sufficiently good evidence itself.
Retained temporal trajectories occupy the intermediate tier of the hierarchy: they are selected when
the current rollout group lacks a verified sibling but useful historical behavior is available in memory.
Their contribution therefore remains smaller and gradually becomes rare. The occasional later spikes
in the fallback tiers correspond to isolated batches in which verified siblings are unavailable, rather
than a reversal of the broader movement toward self-supported supervision.

Figure 7(c) clarifies why temporal supervision remains useful after current adaptation has mostly
converged. The current JSD falls rapidly toward zero as the unassisted and privileged predictions
become aligned at the same policy state. Temporal JSD starts substantially higher and decays more
slowly, preserving a nontrivial gap long after the current divergence becomes small. This sustained
mismatch is precisely what temporal consolidation is meant to preserve: cached distributions remain
fixed while the policy continues to evolve, so the student continues to receive a historical correction
signal until those targets are refreshed. At the same time, the cache signature entropy Hsig rises
during early memory formation and then stabilizes. Since the signature on these tasks is the predicted
option, the plateau near ln 4 indicates that the memory remains balanced across answer options rather
than collapsing toward a single one.

The memory underlying this process is shown directly in Figure 7(d). The cache does not start empty:
before the first update it is seeded with reference trajectories and sparse teacher targets, so temporal
consolidation is active from the beginning of training. From this initial pool, the cache grows steadily
within each task as new verified behaviors are admitted or existing entries are replaced, while the
number of ready entries closely tracks the total count. This close coupling indicates that most retained
experiences quickly become usable temporal targets. The gradual flattening of the averaged curve
as tasks complete simply reflects that finished runs hold their final cache size while the remaining
tasks continue to grow. The narrowing variance band likewise reflects the convergence of cache sizes
across tasks as each run reaches its dataset limit.

Figure 8 translates this expansion into actual memory cost. As the number of retained entries
increases, the footprint grows gradually from the seeded cache at step 1 to 942 mean entries and
4.8 MB across five tasks. The growth remains small because each entry stores only sparse function-
space information, including trajectory tokens, a top-K teacher distribution, and pooled tail mass,
rather than any model-sized state. Each task’s cache size is non-decreasing; completed runs are held
at their final value so the averaged curve reflects cumulative growth across all five tasks. Even at its
peak, the cache occupies only about 5 MB of CPU memory, confirming that the temporal memory
remains negligible relative to the resident model state.

The effect of these mechanisms on policy quality becomes visible in Figure 7(e). Both mean rollout
reward and solve rate improve sharply during the early phase, but the solve rate rises faster and
saturates sooner. This separation follows from how the two quantities are defined: a problem is
counted as solved once one successful rollout appears within its group, whereas mean reward averages
performance across all G sampled trajectories. Early in training, the policy can therefore discover one
correct trajectory while still producing several unsuccessful alternatives, yielding a high solve rate but
a lower average reward. As training progresses, successful behavior becomes more consistent across
the rollout group, narrowing this gap and reducing the variance across tasks. The resulting plateau
indicates that rapid early improvement gives way to a stable regime rather than continued oscillation.

Finally, Figure 7(f) shows how the quality of retained behavior and generation structure evolve
together. Mean cache reward rises rapidly as stronger verified trajectories replace weaker initial
memories and then approaches a stable high-quality regime. Active-token length follows a different
pattern:  it exhibits a brief spike at the very beginning, when the unadapted policy can generate
substantially longer trajectories, but then quickly contracts toward its steady-state level and remains
stable thereafter. This sharp early correction suggests that the policy is learning to produce more
task-sufficient responses rather than simply generating longer ones. Together with the rise in cache
reward, this indicates that improved solution quality emerges alongside increasingly compact and
stable generations, rather than being driven by uncontrolled expansion of trajectory length.
