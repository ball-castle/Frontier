                                               Abstract

             We show that multimodal models possess strong reasoning abilities and that an appropriate har-2026                   ness can unlock their potential to solve tasks across diverse interactive environments. We introduce
                VISTA, a visual harness that gives a general-purpose multimodal model long-horizon vision. VISTA
                   allows the model to directly perceive the environment through visual observations and maintains aOct                      lossless visual memory that preserves past observations in their original form. The model can ac-
1                 tively retrieve these observations and reorganize its visual input as it reasons. On ARC-AGI-3,
               VISTA improves Claude Opus 5.0’s Relative Human Action Efficiency score from 40.68 to a per-
                      fect 100.00, with the model completing all 25 public games using 57.4% fewer actions than first-time
               human participants. VISTA’s simple design also allows it to extend naturally to diverse visual envi-
                  ronments with minimal adaptation. Across three additional benchmarks covering a diverse range of
                     visual games and puzzles, it substantially outperforms baselines using the same underlying model[cs.AI]              with minimal harnesses. Our results highlight VISTA’s potential as a general-purpose visual harness
                      for advancing multimodal agents in complex visual environments.

To this end, we introduce VISTA, a visual harness that allows the model to actively reorganize its visual input dur-
ing long-horizon interactions (Figure 1c). The model receives visual observations of the environment that preserve
the appearance and spatial relations of objects.  It develops its understanding of the environment through free-form
language reasoning [42, 55] and uses this understanding to predict action outcomes and plan subsequent actions [59].
Instead of relying solely on visual representations retained in the model’s context, which may be compressed, lossy,
and insufficient for subsequent reasoning, VISTA maintains a lossless visual memory. This memory stores every frame
returned by the environment, including intermediate animation frames, in its original form. The model can retrieve
observations from this memory through visual inspection, selecting frames to compare and spatial regions to examine
at different levels of detail based on its current reasoning needs [47, 48]. Figure 2 shows two example agent trajectories
with VISTA, illustrating how observation, reasoning, inspection, and action are interleaved.
With VISTA, Claude Opus 5.0 [3] completes all 25 public ARC-AGI-3 games with a Relative Human Action Efficiency
(RHAE) score of 100.00 with xhigh reasoning effort, using 57.4% fewer actions than the first-time human baseline.
VISTA with GPT-5.6 Sol [33] likewise completes all 25 games, achieving an RHAE score of 99.00 with max reasoning
effort. To our knowledge, VISTA is the first vision-based system to achieve perfect or near-perfect performance on
ARC-AGI-3 without program synthesis. For comparison, the organizers’ official baselines, which also do not use
programs, achieve RHAE scores of 40.68 with Opus 5.0 high and 13.33 with GPT-5.6 Sol max [5, 34].
The simplicity of VISTA also allows it to extend naturally to other visual environments with minimal adaptation. We
apply the same visual harness to 34 browser games from GameWorld [35], 10 browser games from AI GameStore [60],
and 39 visual tracking problems from BabyVision [12]. Together, these benchmarks cover a wide range of games and
visual reasoning tasks that require skills such as complex reasoning, spatial navigation, and strategic planning. GPT-
5.6 Sol with VISTA substantially outperforms baselines that use the same model with minimal harnesses.  It also
outperforms the novice human baseline on GameWorld and the human median baseline on AI GameStore. Overall,
these results demonstrate VISTA’s effectiveness across diverse visual environments and highlight visual harness design
as an important direction for improving multimodal agents.

2  Related Work

ARC-AGI benchmarks.  ARC-AGI-1 [14] and ARC-AGI-2 [15] are benchmarks designed to evaluate abstract rea-
soning over static input–output grids [16].  Approaches to these benchmarks often use pretrained large language
models [1, 8, 9, 19, 26, 37], recurrent models [23, 50], or vision-based models [22]. ARC-AGI-3 [4] makes this setting
interactive: an agent must explore novel environments, adapt continuously from its interactions, and take actions to
achieve its goals [24]. Existing approaches to ARC-AGI-3 largely rely on program synthesis [11, 25, 39, 40, 62], in

which agents perceive the environment through textual or symbolic representations and abstract it into programs that
can be executed, verified, and searched over to guide actions. While effective on ARC-AGI-3, these approaches rely on
constructing explicit programmatic abstractions of the environment. As environments become more visually complex,
representing their states, transition dynamics, and goal conditions with explicit programs may become increasingly
difficult. In contrast, VISTA operates directly on visual observations and reasons in natural language.
Computer-use agents.  Recent work has explored agents that interact with computers and graphical user interfaces to
complete complex tasks. Existing approaches broadly differ in their observation space. Text-based approaches operate
over structured representations of the interface, such as HTML, DOM trees, accessibility trees, or element identifiers,
and use language models to reason over these representations and take symbolic actions [17, 32, 64]. Vision-based
computer-use agents interact directly with screenshots and take grounded actions such as clicks, mouse movements,
and keyboard inputs [13, 30, 38, 52, 57]. VISTA follows the vision-based paradigm: the agent reasons directly from
visual observations rather than textual or symbolic representations of the environment. It focuses on a visual-centric
harness for reasoning in interactive worlds and provides a simple interface for agents to observe and interact with the
environment.
Game-playing agents.  Games have long served as testbeds for developing agents that learn to perceive, reason,
and act in interactive environments. Classical approaches learn policies through reinforcement learning [31, 43, 44] or
learn environment dynamics to support prediction and planning [21, 41]. While these methods provide general learning
algorithms, they typically require training a separate agent for each game. More recently, foundation models have
enabled more general-purpose agents that operate in richer game environments. Voyager uses a large language model
together with structured Minecraft observations and an executable skill library [51], while JARVIS-1 incorporates
visual observations and retrieves similar past experiences for planning in Minecraft [53]. Generalist agents such as
SIMA and SIMA 2 [45, 46], Cradle [49], and Game-TARS [54] further operate directly from visual observations across
multiple environments. A central challenge in these systems is retaining information over long interaction horizons:
prior agents typically maintain only a bounded visual context or selectively store and compress past experiences.
VISTA instead preserves the complete visual interaction history losslessly and allows the agent to actively revisit
arbitrary previous frames and regions during reasoning.

3  VISTA: A Visual Harness

VISTA lets the model directly perceive the environment through visual inputs. However, existing multimodal systems
typically encode visual observations once and discard the raw inputs. As the context fills, these representations may
be removed or replaced by textual summaries, and the model may not control what is retained. More importantly, the
model may not know at encoding time which visual information will matter later: a representation sufficient at one
stage may omit details required in subsequent reasoning steps.
These limitations directly motivate the design of VISTA, which preserves original observations in a lossless visual
memory and provides tools for the model to retrieve and inspect them during interaction. This allows the model to
revisit visual evidence and reorganize its visual input as it reasons, without requiring all relevant information to be
captured in the initial visual encoding and retained in the model’s context.

3.1  Visual Harness Components

We introduce three key visual harness components: visual observations, which allows the agent to perceive the envi-
ronment through rendered images; lossless visual memory, which preserves visual observations in their original form;
and visual inspection, which allows the model to revisit visual evidence and examine details as needed. Together, these
components provide the model with an explicit attention mechanism over the interaction history, allowing it to select
which frames and regions to examine during reasoning.
Visual observation.  The model receives visual observations from the environment (Figure. 3a). These observations
preserve the appearance and spatial relation of objects, allowing the model to use its learned visual priors to interpret
and reason about the environment.
Lossless visual memory.  The harness stores every frame returned by the environment, including intermediate ani-
mation frames, in its original form (Figure. 3b). Each frame is indexed by turn and frame number and remains available
throughout the interaction, independently of what is retained in the model’s current context.
Visual inspection.  Building on the visual memory, we provide the agent with tools to actively inspect visual infor-
mation in space and across time (Figure 3c). In space, it can request an enlarged view of any rectangular region from

3.2  Pipeline

In our pipeline, the model and the harness interact through a multi-turn conversation (Figure 2). The harness is purely
mechanical and does not involve any trained model: it executes tool calls requested by the model, returns outputs such
as images or pixel values, and reports errors for invalid requests. The model reasons over these outputs and issues its
next request to the harness if needed.
A single agent interacts with each game throughout the run. Each game turn may include multiple exchanges with the
harness before the agent executes one game action (Figure 3d). At the start of a turn, the agent receives the current
visual state and available actions.  It can also consult two notes that it maintains: GUIDE.md, which records its
understanding of the game and information that may remain useful across levels, and WORKING.md, which serves as
a scratchpad for the current state. Through inspect, the agent can revisit earlier observations, including intermediate
animation frames produced by its previous actions. It can also use read pixels to examine pixel values within a
selected region. Consulting the notes and using the inspection tools are optional and left to the agent’s decision.
The agent uses free-form language to reason and plan. It predicts the outcome of its planned action, revises its notes
to reflect its updated understanding of the environment, and calls play to execute the chosen action and advance to
the next game turn. When its context approaches the limit, the agent writes a concise handoff summary and resumes
from the current state in a fresh context. Its notes (GUIDE.md and WORKING.md), visual memory, and action history
remain available.

read pixels, and play), together with their pseudocode and corresponding descriptions are provided to the agent,
as shown in Appendix A.4).

4  VISTA on ARC-AGI-3

4.1  Setup

Dataset.  ARC-AGI-3 [4] consists of interactive visual games whose rules and objectives are not provided explicitly.
We evaluate on the 25 public games. At each step, the agent observes the current visual state, selects an available
action, and uses the resulting observation to infer object roles, transition rules, hidden states, and possible goals. Each
game contains multiple levels that share underlying mechanics while introducing new concepts, objects, layouts, or
constraints. The agent therefore needs to retain and apply previously inferred knowledge across levels while continu-
ally adapting its understanding through interaction. Figure 4 shows an example agent trajectory.
Evaluation metrics.  Performance is measured by Relative Human Action Efficiency (RHAE) [6], which compares
an agent’s action efficiency with that of first-time human players. A completed level receives 100% credit when its
action count matches the human baseline, up to 115% when it uses fewer actions, and partial credit when it uses more
actions. Unfinished levels receive zero credit. Later levels receive greater weight, and each game score is capped by
the weighted fraction of levels completed. See Appendix A.3 for details.
Models.  We instantiate VISTA with two general-purpose, closed-weight multimodal model backends commonly
used in prior work. Each backend is evaluated independently under the same interaction protocol. We use Claude
Opus 5.0 [3] run with extra-high reasoning effort through the Claude Code CLI and GPT-5.6 Sol [33] run with max
reasoning effort through the Codex CLI.

4.4  Qualitative Analysis

The key components of the VISTA harness are lossless visual memory and visual inspection, which allow the agent to
revisit stored visual information at any point during interaction. These components provide a form of explicit attention
over interaction history. Here, we show qualitative examples of how agents benefit from this re-attention mechanism.
Figure 10a illustrates re-attention across space and time. The agent revisits earlier frames to recover information that
is no longer visible in its current observation. In this example, it inspects frames from different points in the trajectory
and integrates their information to reconstruct the overall map. Figure 10b illustrates re-attention to local visual details.
The agent selectively zooms into a small region of a stored observation to determine a block’s orientation, which is
difficult to see clearly in the full 512×512 frame. Together, these examples illustrate two complementary forms of re-
attention enabled by lossless visual memory: integrating visual evidence across the interaction history and inspecting
local details at the granularity required by the current task.

5  General Visual Environments

VISTA’s general-purpose design allows us to naturally apply the same visual harness to other benchmarks with minimal
adaptation. We evaluate VISTA on two interactive game benchmarks: GameWorld [35] and AI GameStore [60]. We
also evaluate on a static visual reasoning benchmark, BabyVision [12], to examine the role of visual inspection without
interaction with a dynamic environment. For each benchmark, we compare VISTA with the official implementation
using the same model. We also report human performance and the best previously reported result as references. See
Appendices A.3 and A.6 for details. Figure 11 shows an overview of these environments. Appendix Figure 14 shows
example VISTA trajectories on both interactive and static tasks.

5.1  Setup

GameWorld.  GameWorld [35] contains 34 browser games with 170 tasks across five genres: arcade, platformer,
puzzle, runner, and simulation. The agent receives only the official task prompt, available controls, and screenshots.
We evaluate VISTA using the computer-use interface, through which the agent interacts with the games directly via
keyboard and mouse actions. We report success rate (SR), defined as the fraction of the 170 tasks completed, and
progress (PG), which also credits partial completion. Both metrics are computed by the benchmark’s official evaluator.
AI GameStore.  AI GameStore [60] contains 10 public browser games. The agent receives only the public game
description, controls, and visual observations. Through interaction, it must infer the game dynamics and determine
how to achieve the objectives. Each game uses its own scoring system. Following the benchmark’s official protocol, we
normalize each raw score so that the corresponding published human median equals 100, then compute the geometric
mean of the normalized scores across the ten games.
BabyVision.  BabyVision [12] contains 388 static visual reasoning tasks across 22 subclasses. Each task consists of
a single static image and a question. The agent perceives the image and can decide which visual regions to inspect and
which individual pixels to read. We evaluate on the 39 questions from two Visual Tracking subtypes: 20 maze tasks
and 19 connecting-lines tasks. We report accuracy across these tasks.

5.2  Results

Figure 11 shows our results. Across all three benchmarks, VISTA consistently outperforms the corresponding base-
lines with minimal harness. VISTA also exceeds the reported performance of fresh human players on GameWorld and

AI GameStore. On BabyVision, where observations are static and no interaction history is involved, VISTA improves
accuracy from 41.0% with the official implementation to 63.2%, highlighting the benefit of visual inspection, which
allows the agent to selectively inspect regions within a single image.

6  Limitations

We acknowledge that the models’ existing capabilities remain a key factor in this success, and VISTA provides a
simple but effective way to elicit them [18, 58, 61]. Its substantial gains over the baselines highlight visual harness
design as a direction that complements model improvements. Additionally, because the models we use were released
after the public benchmarks, we cannot rule out the possibility that these games were included in their training data.
Evaluation on novel games, such as those in the private set of ARC-AGI-3 [4], would provide a stronger test of our
system’s generalization.

7  Conclusion

VISTA shows that a simple visual harness can unlock the potential of existing multimodal models to complete complex
tasks in interactive visual environments.  It provides the model with raw visual observations, preserves them in a
lossless visual memory, and allows the model to revisit past visual experience at the original level of detail during
interaction. We show that this simple design is sufficient for the agent to complete all 25 public ARC-AGI-3 games
with a perfect score. The same harness extends naturally to other environments covering a diverse set of visual
games and puzzles, with minimal adaptation. Overall, VISTA’s simple design and strong performance across diverse
benchmarks suggest its potential as a general-purpose harness for tasks beyond games. Future work could evaluate
VISTA on more complex and realistic tasks, such as embodied tasks in environments that more closely resemble the
physical world [10].

A  Experimental Details

A.1  Baselines: Official Implementations

For each benchmark, we use the authors’ reported results when available. Otherwise, we rerun the official implemen-
tation following the reported agent configuration. We use the models through their subscription-based CLI interfaces
(Claude Opus 5.0 through the Claude Code CLI and GPT-5.6 Sol through the Codex CLI) rather than through direct
API calls. For the open-weight GLM-5.3 Flash 320B , we also use the Codex CLI, with GLM as the model backend.

ARC-AGI-3.  On ARC-AGI-3, the organizers’ minimal agent interface represents each observation as a textual grid
and provides a list of available actions. When an action produces multiple frames, it retains up to seven intermediate
states. It maintains a 175K-token context window; once the window is exceeded, the earliest turns are discarded, so the
agent has no long-term memory. We limit the number of actions per game to 2,000 for Claude Opus 5.0 and GPT-5.6
Sol, and to 3,000 for GLM-5.3 Flash 320B. Table 1 reports the organizers’ published results for Claude Opus 5.0 and
GPT-5.6 Sol [5, 7, 34]. Figure 8 shows our results for GLM-5.3 Flash 320B, obtained using the original codebase.

GameWorld.  On GameWorld [35], we reproduce the official computer-use agent with GPT-5.6 Sol, following the
official interaction and evaluation protocols. The agent operates directly from screenshots using keyboard and mouse
actions, with each model request including the screenshots, reasoning, and actions from the two preceding steps. Each
task is limited to 100 actions.

AI GameStore.  On AI GameStore [60], we reproduce the official evaluation loop. The agent makes one decision
per game second, receiving five frames sampled at 0.2 s intervals. Each model request includes only these current
frames as visual input, together with the scratchpad, action, and result from the preceding decision. A run has 120
decisions, with the game paused during model inference.

BabyVision.  On BabyVision [12], the baseline is the official one-shot setting: the model receives the image and
question in a single request and answers without additional visual inspection or interaction. We extract the final
answer using the benchmark’s reference code.

A.2  Ablation Details

We incrementally modify the official ARC-AGI-3 implementation to examine the contributions of VISTA’s compo-
nents (Figure 5). The official implementation accesses the model through the API, whereas our experiments use the
Codex CLI through the subscription interface. Therefore, the officially reported result should be treated as an external
reference. However, we use the same underlying model and reasoning effort to match the official configuration as
closely as possible. All configurations (i)–(vi) use the same model access interface and are directly comparable with
one another.
(i): Image observations. We first replace the textual grid observations with rendered images. This leads to an RHAE
score of 47.32, demonstrating the importance of visual observations. We hypothesize that improved token efficiency
contributes to this gain: each image observation requires approximately 308 image tokens, compared with roughly
4K text tokens for an equivalent 64 × 64 textual grid. A request containing seven frames therefore uses roughly 28K
tokens for textual observations, compared with about 2.2K for images. Image observations leave more of the model’s
limited context window available for reasoning within each request.
(i)→(ii): Increased action and time limits. The official implementation uses a per-section action limit of 5× the
human baseline and a 12-hour runtime limit. We find that many runs terminate upon reaching these limits, leaving the
games unfinished. We therefore increase the limits to 2,000 total actions and 48 hours per game, improving the RHAE
score to 51.66. We do not observe substantial additional benefits from further increasing these limits.
(ii)→(iii): Continuous conversation. We next allow the agent to interact with the environment through a continuous
conversation. In the official implementation, each model request produces at most one environment action, and the
interaction history is provided again with the next request. When this history exceeds the context limit, the oldest
interactions are truncated, leaving only a sliding window of recent experience. Our setting allows multiple play
calls within the same conversation and uses native context compaction when the context limit is reached. This pre-
serves a compressed representation of earlier interactions, allowing information from past turns to remain available
for subsequent reasoning.

(iii)→(iv): Add notes.  We introduce a minimal memory mechanism by adding two note  files: GUIDE.md
and WORKING.md.  GUIDE.md stores reusable knowledge about the game across levels and sessions, while
WORKING.md stores temporary state and plans for the current level. When the context approaches its limit, the
agent writes a handoff summary in WORKING.md before resuming in a fresh context. These notes remain available
across context boundaries, allowing the agent to carry forward its understanding of the game and its current state.
(iv)→(vi): Add VISTA’s visual harness. Finally, we add VISTA’s key visual harness components: lossless visual
memory, model-directed inspection (inspect), and exact pixel readout (read pixels). The agent receives only
the final frame after each action and can decide when to use these tools to revisit its visual memory as it reasons. These
additions complete the VISTA harness.

A.4  Agent Tools

The agent accesses tools only through structured function calls; plain text generated by the model cannot trigger tool
execution. In Claude Code, we expose these tools through an MCP server and disable all built-in tools, preventing
direct shell or file-system access. In Codex, we register the same tools directly with the app server. In both cases, the
agent runs in a sandboxed container without access to environment credentials.
A single host-side controller handles all tool calls. The controller maintains the environment connection, archived
frames, GUIDE.md, and WORKING.md, and is the only component allowed to advance the game.  It also enforces
sequential interaction: a new play call is accepted only after the result of the previous action has been returned to the
agent. Each returned image is labeled with its turn and frame index. Claude Code receives these images directly in the
MCP result. In our Codex integration, tool outputs are text-only, and the controller provides the corresponding images
separately as part of the same turn.
When the context is nearly full, the controller pauses normal tool use until the agent saves the state needed to con-
tinue. In Codex, the agent uses save compact checkpoint; in Claude Code, a pre-compaction hook requests a
write working call. The agent then resumes in a fresh context containing GUIDE.md, WORKING.md, the action
history, and the current frame.

The VISTA tool interface in pseudocode. play is the only tool that changes the game environment, note-writing
tools update only the agent’s stored notes, and all other tools are read-only.

Each benchmark uses a short prompt following the same template, differing only in benchmark-specific instructions
for the action space, task objective, and notes.

ARC-AGI-3.  The same prompt is used for every ARC-AGI-3 game.

Prompt 1 ARC-AGI-3.


# Visual game task

Complete the game with as few game actions as possible.

Build and use a compact, revisable model of the game and its current state. Update
it as new evidence changes what is supported.

Before each ‘play‘, briefly state what you expect to see. Afterward, briefly state
all visible changes, expected or not.

Keep concise, durable, revisable game understanding in ‘GUIDE.md‘; use ‘WORKING.md‘
as a scratchpad when useful.


GameWorld and AI GameStore.  GameWorld and AI GameStore use the same environment-neutral prompt. We

Prompt 2 GameWorld and AI GameStore.


# Interactive world task

Pursue the supplied objective.

Build and use a compact, revisable model of the environment and its current state.
Update it as new evidence changes what is supported.

Before each ‘act‘, briefly state what you expect to observe. Afterward, briefly
state all observable changes, expected or not.

Keep concise, durable, revisable understanding in ‘GUIDE.md‘; use ‘WORKING.md‘ as a

Build a short, revisable account of what the image shows and update it as the

A.6  Human References

ARC-AGI-3.  The organizers’ study involved 486 participants from the general public with diverse demographics.
Participants were not selected for specialized training or skills [4]. Each game was tested by ten participants encoun-
tering it for the first time, without task-specific instructions. Testing took place in 90-minute sessions, with a soft time
limit of 20 minutes and a hard cutoff of 30 minutes per game. A game was included only if at least two participants
independently completed all levels. For each level, the official RHAE human baseline is the upper-median action
count among first-time human players who completed that level [6].

GameWorld.  GameWorld reports results for two human players, both computer science graduate students, each of
whom attempted all 170 tasks under the same budget of 100 actions per task as the agents [35]. The novice player
had no prior exposure to the games or tasks, while the expert player had studied the rules and practiced the controls
beforehand. We use the novice player’s performance as our reference for first-time human play.

AI GameStore.  The benchmark’s human study recruited 106 participants through Prolific (mean age = 38.81) [60]
to play each game over a customized game interface. Each participant was randomly assigned ten games, and was
given two minutes per game. We use the median score among participants who played each game as the human
reference, normalized to 100 as described above.

BabyVision.  BabyVision’s human evaluation involved 16 adult participants, each holding at least a bachelor’s de-
gree, who completed all 388 questions [12]. The reported human accuracy is 95.0% on the Maze subtype and 89.5%
on Connect the Lines. We compute the human reference in Figure 11, 92.3%, as the weighted mean of these subtype
scores, using the 20 maze and 19 connecting-lines questions in our evaluation.

B  Additional Results

B.1  Run-to-run Variation

We repeat our main evaluations three times to measure run-to-run variation. On ARC-AGI-3, VISTA with GPT-5.6 Sol
at maximum reasoning effort achieves RHAE scores of 99.00, 98.90, and 99.23 across the three runs, corresponding to
99.04 ± 0.17 (mean ± standard deviation). On GameWorld, the three runs achieve success rates of 64.7%, 64.7%, and
60.6%, and progress scores of 81.0%, 80.0%, and 76.9%, corresponding to 63.33 ± 2.37% success rate and 79.30 ±
2.14% progress. On BabyVision, the three runs achieve 61.5%, 61.5%, and 66.7%, corresponding to 63.23 ± 3.00%.
For AI Game Store, the three runs obtain aggregate scores of 119.99, 148.58, and 140.06. We follow the benchmark’s
official multi-run evaluation protocol rather than averaging these three final aggregate scores directly: for each game,
we first average its raw score across the three runs, and then apply the benchmark’s normalization and aggregation
procedure to these per-game means.
