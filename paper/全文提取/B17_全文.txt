A.2. Word embeddings
Corpus. We train all word embedding models on the November 2023 dump of English Wikipedia, downloaded from
https://huggingface.co/datasets/wikimedia/wikipedia. We lowercase the corpus, remove all numer-

We use a vocabulary consisting of the V = 25000 most frequently appearing words. This automatically includes the words
corresponding to the continuous concepts we probe (months, years, and US states). We discard out-of-vocabulary words
from the corpus; our robustness checks indicated that it does not practically matter whether out-of-vocabulary words are
removed or simply masked. Ultimately, the training corpus comprises 2.72 billion tokens.

distance-based reweighting function), the co-occurrence (skip-gram) distribution Pij of corpus C is defined

where C[ν] is the ν-th token in the corpus, δ is the Kronecker delta, and Z is simply the normalizing constant. We choose

B. Review of word embedding algorithms
In this appendix we review recent results showing that word embedding algorithms are well-approximated by spectral
methods. We first review the mechanism by which symmetric word embeddings trained via self-supervised gradient-based
algorithms learn the spectral decomposition of the positive part of the co-occurrence matrix M ⋆. We then discuss how the
PCA geometry of asymmetric word embeddings recovers instead the matrix absolute value |M ⋆|. Finally, we discuss the
relation between M ⋆and the well-known pointwise mutual information (PMI) matrix.

B.1. Symmetric word embeddings from M ⋆
In the main text, we use W to denote the trained embeddings. Since we are now discussing optimization, we change
notation, using W to instead refer to the variable being optimized, and Wˆ  to denote the optimal value.

Let the co-occurrence (skip-gram) distribution Pij and the unigram distribution be defined as in Section A.2. Let V be the
number of words in the vocabulary, and let W ∈RV ×d be a trainable weight matrix whose ith row is the d-dimensional
embedding vector for word i. Word embedding algorithms such as word2vec aim to imbue W with semantic structure so
that the inner products between word embeddings capture semantic similarity. In particular, these self-supervised algorithms
iterate over the text corpus, aligning the embeddings of frequently co-occurring words and repelling unrelated words.

Karkada et al. (2025) show that the following symmetric matrix factorization problem well-approximates word2vec,
producing word embeddings of comparable linear algebraic structure and semantic quality:

                Wˆ = arg min ∥W W ⊤−M ⋆∥2F,                                      (20)
                         W

where the target matrix M ⋆is defined as in Equation (1):

Intuitively, M ⋆encodes the normalized excess co-occurrences of word pairs; M (ij)⋆ > 0 when words i and j co-occur
more frequently than if the words were drawn independently, and vice versa. Some additional properties of M ⋆include
that its elements are bounded (−2 ≤M(ij)⋆  ≤2) and that it is real symmetric (and therefore has a real-valued spectral
decomposition M ⋆= ΦΛΦ⊤). The approximation in the last line shows that M ⋆is approximately equal to the pointwise
mutual information (PMI) matrix, elementwise; we derive and discuss it in Appendix B.4.

Karkada et al. (2025) show that running the word2vec algorithm is approximately equivalent to running gradient descent
on the objective in Equation (20), which is known to converge to Wˆ with high probability. By the Eckart-Young-Mirsky
theorem, the trained embeddings must have the structure

                  Wˆ = Φ(·,:d) q Λ(:d,:d)                                            (24)

up to semantically-irrelevant right orthogonal transformations. In other words, W learns to model the d largest positive
eigenmodes of M ⋆.

A limitation of this analysis is that it is restricted to symmetric factorizations. This prevents W from ever learning
eigenmodes of M ⋆with negative eigenvalues. To be precise, let us define the positive semidefinite and negative semidefinite
components of M ⋆, M + and M −respectively, to be the unique matrices satisfying

       M ⋆= M + −M −    M + ⪰0    M −⪰0    M +M −= 0.              (25)

Then, even if W W ⊤has no rank constraint, one may only achieve W W ⊤= M + ̸= M ⋆in general.

B.2. Asymmetric word embeddings from M ⋆
To circumvent this limitation, we consider the asymmetric setting

                                         (Wˆ , Wˆ ′) = arg min ∥W W ′⊤−M ⋆∥2F,                                  (26)

where W is the word embedding matrix and W ′ is known as the context embedding matrix. Unlike the case of symmetric

a substantially larger invariance: (Wˆ , Wˆ ′) ≡(Wˆ A, Wˆ ′A−⊤) for any invertible matrix A. Therefore the geometric
structure of the learned word embeddings Wˆ  is identifiable only up to arbitrary linear transformations.

initialization scheme: W (0) initialized with i.i.d. Gaussian elements as usual, and W ′(0) = QW (0) with random

B.3. Weaker version of Assumption 3.1
Note that if M ⋆is positive semi-definite, then M −= 0, and the result Equation (32) is exactly the same as in the symmetric
case. In particular, the gram matrix exactly recovers M ⋆. This convenience is why we include the PSD assumption in
Assumption 3.1.

However, this assumption is violated in real data. Indeed, the empirical spectrum of M ⋆on Wikipedia has a unimodal
distribution peaked near λ = 0, and rank M + ≈rank M −(Figure 7). Therefore, in Figures 1 and 2, the exponential
kernel estimated by fitting to M ⋆does not, strictly speaking, represent the kernel that is actually factored (which should be
measured from |M ⋆|S instead). This explains why we typically do not accurately predict the absolute scale of the mode
amplitudes. Despite this, the predictions of Corollary 2 and Proposition 3 appear to correctly predict both the relative
amplitudes between modes and the wavenumbers.

To develop theoretical results that more faithfully model real data, we propose the following drop-in replacement for
Assumption 3.1:

Assumption B.1. Translation symmetry of |M ⋆| with kernel C(·). Define M ⋆as in Equation (1), and define its unique
decomposition into PSD and NSD components M ⋆= M + −M −as in Equation (25). Let C+(·) and C−(·) both be
well-behaved functions. For any two words i, j ∈S, assume

         M(ij)+ = C+(dist(xi, xj))    and  M(ij)− = C−(dist(xi, xj))                      (34)

so that

                    |M ⋆|(ij) = C+(dist(xi, xj)) + C−(dist(xi, xj))                              (35)
                       = C(dist(xi, xj))                                                    (36)

where C(·) := C+(·) + C−(·) is the co-occurrence convolution kernel.

Assumption B.1 implies that both |M ⋆|S and MS⋆ have a translation symmetry (with different convolution kernels). Note
that Assumption B.1 strictly softens Assumption 3.1, which corresponds to the special case M −= 0.  In addition,
Assumption B.1 is well-supported in both the empirical data (Figures 5 and 6) and in the collective model of Section 4.1. In
particular, if the function g is large enough in magnitude, the non-linear effects associated with the logarithm in Equation (15)
can lead to both positive and negative values µk.
However, estimating the kernel C+ + C−from the raw data statistics requires computing |M ⋆|S. This involves a global
spectral transformation of the co-occurrence probabilities for all words. This contrasts estimating the kernel C+ −C−
from M S,⋆ which only requires knowledge of co-occurrence probabilities and unigram probabilities for the words in S.
In this sense, the approximate Assumption 3.1 is easier to use than Assumption B.1. For this reason, we choose to use
Assumption 3.1 when producing theoretical predictions in Figures 1 and 2.

C.1. Helper-based reconstruction
Here we provide additional data supporting the helper-based reconstruction. In Figure 15, we show the reconstruction task
PMI (as described in A.1.4) using non-seasonal “number” words ranging from “one” to “seventeen,” which do not exhibit
seasonal modulation. The reconstructed PMI and embeddings from this task show that the months are not correctly ordered,
consistent with the notion that the numbers do not vary in seasonality and therefore fail to assist in reconstruction.

We can identify the affinity of each word in the vocabulary with the month embeddings as a proxy for their seasonality. We
define the month-word affinity as A = QwordsQ†months ∈RV ×12 where Qmonths ∈R12×K for embedding dimension K
and vocabulary size V . By weighting each month’s affinity by ei2πm/12 for month m, we can extract a seasonality score
from the phase of each word’s resulting complex affinity. Sorting words by the magnitude of the complex affinity (we take
large magnitude to indicate a clear seasonal signal), we select the top-100 seasonal words and perform the reconstruction
task again. As shown in Figure 16, these words produce a very high quality reconstruction.

In Figure 17, we show the reconstruction error versus the number of helper words H used in the reconstruction task, where
the helpers are chosen from the top-100 seasonal words or randomly from the vocabulary. We see that the reconstruction
error decreases as we increase the number of helper words, and that the top seasonal words yield a faster scaling and lower                                √
reconstruction error than random words, with an error scaling as 1/ H.

D. Combined Model of Seasonality and Binary Semantic Attributes
In Section 4.1, we consider words sharing a continuous latent attribute, but lacking any other additional semantic information.
This is an unrealistic assumption, and predicts that the PCA dimensions are strictly Fourier.  Here, we now enrich
the vocabulary by assigning to each word both a seasonal phase x ∈{0, . . . , N −1} and a binary attribute vector
a = (a1, . . . , ad) ∈{±1}d, where the binary attributes encode additional semantic information (e.g. gender, singular vs.
plural, royal vs non-royal, etc...), as in Korchinski et al. (2025). The total vocabulary size is N2d.


Multiplicative generative model.  Seasonality and semantic attributes are assumed to influence co-occurrence indepen-
dently. The seasonal factor is the same as in Section 4:

                                          Ftime(x, y) = 1 + C(tx −ty),

E. Proofs
E.1. Latent semantic lattice
Let S be any subset of the vocabulary with cardinality |S| = LD, for positive integers L and D. To define the latent semantic
lattice, we first define the centered index set N as

                    ({−L2 , −L2 + 1, . . . , L2 −1}D,   L even,
                 N :=                                                                          (46)
                             {−L−12   , −L−12 + 1, . . . , L−12 }D, L odd.

This defines a simple integer lattice in D dimensions with L sites along each axis; thus |N| = LD.

For each word i ∈S, let i 7→ni be a bijective map from a word to its lattice index. Define the latent semantic continuum
coordinate for word i as xi := 2ni/L. Thus the latent coordinates constitute a lattice on the interval [−1, 1]D.

E.1.1. WAVEVECTORS FOR PERIODIC BC
With periodic boundary conditions, we may define the associated reciprocal lattice. We begin with the auxiliary notion of a
positive half-space: we write n ≻0 if the first nonzero component of n is positive, i.e.,

             n ≻0 ⇐⇒∃j ∈{1, . . . , D} such that n1 = · · · = nj−1 = 0 and nj > 0.                  (47)


Next, for each n ∈N we define the periodized negation ⊖n ∈N to be the unique element satisfying

                     ⊖n ≡−n  (mod L)  (componentwise).                                  (48)


Then we may define the following disjoint sets:

                     K+ := {πn : n ∈N and ⊖n ̸= n and n ≻0},                               (49)

                      K−:= {⊖k : k ∈K+},                                                       (50)

                              Ksc := {πn : n ∈N \ {0} and ⊖n = n},                                    (51)

                         K0 := {0}.                                                                   (52)

Their union comprises the set of wavevectors allowed by the periodic boundary conditions:

                        K := K+ ∪K−∪Ksc ∪K0                                       (53)
                        = {πni : i ∈S}.                                                   (54)

Despite being constructed from the same index set, the lattice coordinates {xi}i and the wavevectors {kµ}µ are not
commensurate. The coordinates occupy a lattice in real space whereas the wavevectors live on a lattice in reciprocal space.

Each wavevector k ∈K is associated with the plane wave mode exp ik⊤x  . K+ and K−correspond to ± mode pairs, Ksc
corresponds to nonzero self-conjugate modes, and K0 corresponds to the constant (DC) mode. When L is odd, the only
self-conjugate wavevector is k = 0, so Ksc = ∅; when L is even there are 2D −1 nonzero self-conjugate modes, which lie
on the faces of the Brillouin zone and correspond to plane waves at the Nyquist frequency of the lattice.

Define P := |K+| and S := |Ksc|. We choose an ordering µ 7→kµ of the wavevectors K such that

                 K = {k2p−1, k2p}Pp=1 ∪{k2P +s}Ss=1 ∪{k2P +S+1},                            (55)

with
                 k2p−1 ∈K+,     k2p = ⊖k2p−1 ∈K−,    k2P +s ∈Ksc,    k2P +S+1 = 0.                (56)

Thus K contains first the conjugate pairs (in arbitrary order), followed by the nonzero self-conjugate modes (arbitrary order),
followed by the zero mode.
